Chapter 19 — One Look

Thursday, ten to twelve, the envelopes were out along the alley wall behind Menya Kuro before anyone had to be told. One shop had taped its envelope to the shutter at eye height, flap folded ready, as if it had been doing this for years instead of three days.

The roof at noon had stopped being a decision. Gao Qiang took the rail beside Sato Kenjiro and the two of them watched the corner run on the hour Endo had taught it.

The sedan swung into the alley at two minutes to twelve and sat. At twelve exactly the driver's door opened, and Endo came around to the trunk with the hard case, and counted the way he did crosswords — in ink, in order, nothing twice. Mori held the bag and looked at nothing, which was his job now. Okada paid last, envelope in his left hand, the splint riding high on the right.

"He's faster left-handed than Tuesday," Sato said. "Give him a month, he won't miss the other one."

Under the awning across the way, the stool stood turned toward the pachinko hall, empty again. Nobody on the corner wondered where Hasegawa was. That was the change, finished.

"Nothing for me to carry," Sato said. "Again."

"Good."

In the afternoon Gao Qiang rode two stops north with nothing in his hands, to stand in a room that answered to a page with their name on it. The alley mouth lay bare where the cart would plant itself at six. The print shop had its roller up, solvent and warm paper breathing into the lane, and the older of the two machine men looked over at the sound of the steel door and raised two fingers — hello, or the price of the pallets still being fair; with him it was the same gesture.

Kojima Haruto had cardboard down over the concrete and was working the length of the room in socks, quiet as the machines on the other side of the wall. The dead cooler stood at the far end with a strip of packing tape across its lid like a patient told to hold still.

"How's the body?" Gao Qiang asked.

"It falls the same way every time now." Kojima rolled the shoulder, reset his feet. "That's the whole skill it's got. I'll take it."

A broom leaned in the corner where Sato had left it. The room was swept. Nothing in it said what it was for, which was the point of the room.

---

Thursday night ran long. The week had one risk left in it and the risk had a schedule: the second bell, one o'clock, the Goose. Gao Qiang kept the stove light on and let the hour go — the pachinko floors releasing their people in waves, the smoke from under the window thinned to a thread.

Kenji came in at twenty past one, hung his jacket, and sat down with the pocket book before he touched his shoes.

"One o'clock," he said.

"To the second?"

"To the second." The pencil moved. "Second bell, same as the first. Nakamura paid it out himself at the register, both hands on the tray, and watched the door after the sedan left. He didn't say anything. He's been not saying anything for ten days now."

"And the drawer?"

"Short exactly what it should be short. Nothing else moved." He closed the book. "Pool stands at one twenty-seven until Saturday does its work."

"Then it was a clean week."

"A quiet number is still a number," Kenji said, and finally took his shoes off, and Gao Qiang went to the futon with the week down to one day of hours, and the hours named.

---

Friday morning, Keio. He signed the second column and dated it, and Park Soo-jin's pencil made its small mark and moved on without pausing, and that was the arrangement holding, the clock holding at three.

Ogawa turned up at the vending machines with two cans and pushed one into his hand. "You look almost rested," he said. "It's unsettling. Drink this and tell me nothing."

The folder hour was his last hour on campus, in a carrel where nobody stood behind him. He set the folder down and took the two pages out of the inside pocket and opened them flat.

One page was registry print: Hakuun Advisory Y.K., limited liability company, an address in Roppongi, fourth floor. The consulting line sat in the folder where it always sat — six point four million a quarter, nine quarters running — and nobody at THI could say what it bought.

The other page was his own hand-copying from a dead weekly's archive: a name the story had tied to Kuroda, an estate shop the man ran, and a list of its buildings. This address was on the list. The story was two summers dead.

He read both pages twice, folded them the way they were folded, and put them back. The rest of the folder went into his bag unopened. Today the digging was a street.

Toyoko line to Nakameguro, Hibiya line to Roppongi, and up into early afternoon light with the hills and the crossing noise in three languages.

The address was a side street off the crossing, between a gallery between shows and a parking structure. Six floors of beige tile and smoked glass, doing its best to look like the buildings on either side of it, and succeeding.

He bought a coffee at the stand on the corner and took the spot everyone took — the rail by the smoking area, where a man with a cup and twenty minutes is nobody. Old habit, his father's: before you stand anywhere, find who's paid to notice. The street offered a camera over the gallery door that hadn't been cleaned in a year, the koban's light at the crossing two blocks east, and nobody else. He gave the camera his back and the cup the rest.

The plaque by the entrance said MEIWA BUILDING MANAGEMENT in brushed metal, and under it a string of managed addresses. The name matched his hand-copying letter for letter. The story was two summers dead. The plaque was polished. That was all a plaque could ever tell him — but a dead story that survived onto live metal was a different weight of fact than one that hadn't.

Through the lobby glass: mailboxes, an elevator, a directory in a case beside the inner door. The angle and the reflection gave him floor numbers and nothing else. The fourth floor carried two lines he couldn't read, from any stance a coffee cup explained.

In half an hour the door admitted four people — two lanyards, a dolly of box files, a bicycle helmet — and gave up a woman with a cleaning cart. Where any of them went, the auto-lock kept.

The fourth floor ran the width of the building with its blinds down. One window near the far end had its blinds tilted open a hand's width, and behind the tilt a light burned against the afternoon.

While he watched, a shape crossed the light. Not a face. A shoulder, going somewhere inside the floor at the pace of a man with nobody to hurry for.

It could have been a cleaner. Lights stay on; buildings have caretakers. He watched anyway, because the window was the only part of the fourth floor the street could see, and you watch what you can see.

He let the name come the whole way out, once, there at the rail — the way you take a weight in your hand for one breath, just to know it again. Zhang Wei. A company bled white, a father behind visiting glass, a mother in the ground, and here at the end of it a hand's width of blind on an ordinary building on an ordinary street. Then he set the name down and drank.

Twenty minutes was what a coffee bought. He spent them and not one more, put the cup through the stand's return, and walked the block once the other way to see what the street looked like leaving. It looked like nothing. One look, in daylight, from the street. Home before the light went, because that was the plan, and the plan was the only thing in Roppongi he owned.

---

Friday night, Mei came up after ten with the staff pot wrapped in a towel, and the kitchen filled with steam and the four of them and the two keys still hanging on their wire by the stove.

"The building," Kojima said, ladle in hand.

"Stood where I said I'd stand. It kept its mouth shut." Gao Qiang took his bowl. "Buildings do."

Kojima grunted and ate. Mei looked at him for a second over the pot — one second, no question in it — and passed the towel to Sato.

Kenji laid Saturday out the way he counted a drawer. "Ten o'clock, the rent. Mei's thirteen lands by noon. The Goose opens at six; my wage comes off the till at opening, like always. After dark, the envelope, on its own — nothing else on that street for us that night." He didn't open the book; he didn't need it. "One hundred twenty-three thousand five hundred, Sunday morning."

"Thin," Gao Qiang said.

"Upright," Kenji said.

"Room's swept," Kojima said. "I'll be in it by ten."

"I'll walk the mouths at eleven," Sato said.

Nobody had assigned either of them anything. Gao Qiang nodded and let the plan stand as theirs.

Afterward the futons went down one by one and the apartment folded into its night sounds — Sato's breathing first, always, the radiator's tick, the last train pulling the blocks along behind it. Gao Qiang stayed at the stove light with the two pages flat on the table.

He had come to this city with a name burned into him and no shape to put it in. Now there was a shape: a floor with its blinds down in the middle of a workday, a plaque that matched a dead story, a stripe of light with a shoulder in it. He took the pencil and wrote the plaque's name on the back of the first page, small, and under it, fourth floor — everything the street had told him in one place, in the pocket that already carried the rest of it. One fact moved from his head to the paper, and the paper went back inside his jacket, and that was the whole record of the day.

He thought for one breath about the first card he had ever dealt in this city — a thousand yen across a midnight booth to a man whose hands were shaking, because his own weren't. Sunday was the same bet, made from a stronger position.

Doors keep. Sunday, their own opened first.
