 Chapter 4 — Paper Trail

The Golden Goose was exactly the kind of place its name suggested: a small, unremarkable bar on a side street in Kabukicho that existed to extract money from people who were too drunk to count their change. The sign featured a goose rendered in neon that buzzed at a frequency designed to trigger headaches. The interior was brown — brown counter, brown stools, brown wallpaper that might have been gold once, in the same way that Nakamura Tetsuo might have been handsome once, before thirty years of shochu and rage compressed his face into a permanent scowl.

Kenji walked in at 7 PM.

The bar was quiet — too early for the real crowd, too late for the daytime drinkers. A single patron sat at the far end of the counter, face down in an ashtray. Behind the bar, a heavyset man with a scar on his left thumb was polishing glasses with the mechanical rhythm of someone who had stopped thinking about it years ago.

"We're not open," the man said without looking up.

"I'm not here to drink." Kenji placed his briefcase on the counter. It was the same briefcase he'd carried into the internet cafe — the one with all his worldly possessions, which turned out to be a calculator, three pens, a change of socks, and a printed copy of his CPA certification. "I'm here about your books."

That got the man's attention. He looked up. The scowl deepened. "Who told you I have books?"

"Everyone has books, Nakamura-san. The question is whether yours are accurate." Kenji opened the briefcase and laid the CPA certificate on the counter like a surgeon laying out instruments. "My name is Murakami Kenji. Twenty-five years in corporate accounting. I'm between positions, and I'm offering my services at a rate that I think you'll find... compatible with your current situation."

Nakamura stared at the certificate. Then at Kenji. Then at the certificate again.

"You're that accountant," he said slowly. "The one who got fired from Tanaka."

"The one who was *wrongfully terminated* from Tanaka," Kenji corrected mildly. "Yes. I have no industry references, no prospects, and no desire to return to corporate Japan. What I have is a skill set that includes the ability to organize messy finances, identify missing funds, and prepare documentation that will satisfy any authority — tax office, licensing bureau, or otherwise — that might take an interest in your establishment."

He paused. Let the words land.

"I'm not expensive. I'm not curious about where the money comes from. And I'm very, very good."

Nakamura's scarred thumb stopped mid-polish. The scowl didn't change — it was probably permanent, a feature rather than a bug — but something shifted behind his eyes. Calculation.

"Three thousand an hour," Nakamura said. "Cash. No questions."

"Agreed."

"Start tonight. Back office. Don't touch anything that's not paper."

"Understood."

Nakamura jerked his head toward a door behind the bar. Kenji picked up his briefcase, walked past the counter, and disappeared into the back office.

The office was a crime scene of paper. Receipts stuffed into shoeboxes. Ledgers with entries written in three different handwritings, none of them legible. A laptop that was old enough to vote. The smell of cigarettes and mildew.

Kenji sat down, opened his briefcase, and smiled.

Not the empty smile of a broken man. The sharp, predatory smile of a senior accountant who has just been handed a ten-thousand-piece puzzle and can already see the picture on the box.

He started with the shoeboxes.

---

Four hours later, Kenji emerged from the back office. His sleeves were rolled up, his collar was loosened, and he had a look on his face that Gao Qiang — who was waiting on a bench outside the bar — recognized immediately. It was the look of a man holding a bomb.

"Well?" Gao Qiang asked.

"Nakamura is skimming from Kuroda," Kenji said quietly. "Not much — maybe five percent of the weekly take. But it's systematic. He's been doing it for two years. And the records he's been sending upstream are sloppy enough that Kuroda's people haven't caught it, but detailed enough that anyone who looked would see the gaps."

"You have proof?"

"I have *envelopes*." Kenji's voice was almost reverent. "Two years of discrepancy. Documented, cross-referenced, and organized. One phone call to Kuroda-gumi and Nakamura is swimming in Tokyo Bay with concrete shoes."

Gao Qiang processed this. "We're not calling Kuroda."

"No," Kenji agreed. "We're not. Because Nakamura is more useful alive and afraid."

"Exactly. We hold the information. We don't use it — not yet. We let Nakamura think we're just a cheap accountant. We build trust. And when the time comes—"

"When the time comes, he's already ours."

The system chimed.

Gao Qiang read the failure condition. *Nakamura reports you to Kuroda-gumi.* That wasn't a slap on the wrist. That was a death sentence wrapped in a mission description.

"Kenji," he said. "How long before Nakamura realizes what you've found?"

"Not long. He's not stupid — violent, but not stupid. He'll wonder why I didn't ask questions about the discrepancies. By tomorrow night, he'll be suspicious."

"Then we move before he gets suspicious. Tomorrow, you go back. You finish organizing the books — legitimately, thoroughly, the best work he's ever seen. You make yourself indispensable. And you don't let on that you know about the skimming."

"And the leverage?"

"We hold it. We hold it like a knife behind our back. And we wait for the right moment to use it."

Kenji nodded. This was familiar territory — not the criminal element, but the strategic dance of corporate leverage. The principle was the same whether you were dealing with a board of directors or a bar owner in Kabukicho: *information is only valuable as long as the other person doesn't know you have it.*

They walked back to the apartment in silence. The streets of Kabukicho were coming alive now — neon flickering on, host club boys styling their hair in alleyway mirrors, the mechanical chirp of pachinko parlors opening their doors. The night shift was starting.

Halfway home, Gao Qiang's system pinged again.

Gao Qiang stopped walking. He scanned the street. Host clubs, izakayas, a pharmacy, a love hotel with a neon heart that pulsed like a cardiac monitor.

An alley between the pharmacy and the love hotel. Voices. One loud, one quiet.

The loud voice was Japanese, male, aggressive — the particular tone of someone who enjoyed being cruel. The quiet voice was female, strained, trying to maintain composure while failing.

Gao Qiang looked at Kenji.

Kenji read his face and shook his head. "Big Brother. We have 50 LP. We can't afford to recruit blindly."

"I'm not recruiting blindly. The system brought me here."

"The system brought you *an option*. It didn't bring you an obligation."

Gao Qiang turned and walked toward the alley.

"Big Brother—"

"Stay here."

He turned into the alley. It was narrow, lit by a single fluorescent bulb that buzzed and flickered. Ten meters in, a scene arranged itself in the unsteady light.

A woman — early twenties, maybe younger — was pressed against the wall by a man in a cheap suit. She was wearing a waitress uniform, apron stained, hair escaping from a clip. The man had one hand on the wall beside her head and the other holding her phone out of reach above her.

"Come on, one drink," the man was saying. His breath carried the sour-sweet of cheap beer. "You've been working hard. Let me buy you a drink. Maybe something to eat. My treat."

"I said no," the woman said. Her voice was steady, but her hands were shaking. "Please give me back my phone."

"Give me your number first. Then you get the phone. Fair trade, right?"

Gao Qiang assessed. The man was bigger than him. Not taller — Gao Qiang had height — but heavier, with the soft thickness of someone who drank more than he exercised. No visible weapon. The woman was cornered but not yet touched. This was harassment, not assault. The distinction mattered for what came next.

"Oi."

Both of them looked. The man's face cycled through surprise, irritation, and then the performative aggression of a predator whose territory has been questioned.

"Who the hell are you? This is a private conversation."

"It didn't look private from the street." Gao Qiang kept his voice calm, conversational. His heart was hammering, but twenty-two years of controlling his expression in hostile environments — classrooms, courtrooms, the visiting room of Qingpu Prison — kept his face neutral. "She asked for her phone back. Give it to her."

The man stepped away from the woman, turning fully toward Gao Qiang. He was bigger up close. Thicker neck. The kind of face that had been punched before and had learned nothing from the experience.

"You her boyfriend? Her brother? What — some Chinese guy saving the damsel?" He laughed. It was an ugly sound. "Walk away, *gaijin*. This doesn't concern you."

The system flickered:

Gao Qiang didn't need the system to tell him what to do here. Some things were calculated. Some things were just *right*.

"I'm going to say this once," he said. "Give her the phone. Walk away. Go home and rethink the way you treat women."

The man's face darkened. He stepped forward — close enough that Gao Qiang could smell the beer, the cheap cologne, the sourness of a man who had been told *no* and couldn't process it.

"You want to make me?"

Gao Qiang didn't move. Didn't flinch. He looked the man directly in the eyes and said, in Mandarin-accented Japanese that was somehow clearer and more precise than anything he'd said before:

"Yes."

One word. Flat. Final.

The man blinked. Something in Gao Qiang's eyes — the years of being the Chinese kid in a room full of people who wanted him gone — communicated itself without translation.

The man's hand opened. The phone dropped. The woman caught it on reflex.

The man shoved past Gao Qiang — shoulder check, hard — and stalked out of the alley, muttering something about *fucking foreigners*.

Silence. The fluorescent bulb buzzed.

The woman was staring at Gao Qiang. Her hands were still shaking, but she was holding her phone like a lifeline.

"Are you okay?" Gao Qiang asked.

"I... yes. Thank you." She was breathing hard. Japanese, obviously. Pretty, in the understated way of someone who didn't think of herself that way — round face, dark eyes, the kind of features that were called *plain* by people who weren't paying attention. "He — that's the third time this week. He waits outside the restaurant after my shift."

"Have you reported him?"

She laughed. It was short, humorless. "To who? The police? He hasn't *done* anything. Not technically."

Gao Qiang understood. The system understood too.

Gao Qiang had 50 LP. The recruitment would cost 30, leaving him 20. A razor-thin margin.

But he looked at Tanaka Mei — shaking hands, defiant eyes, a woman holding her own life together with a waitressing job and sheer willpower — and he thought about the system's first lesson: *Every Brother was chosen because their life was broken by forces beyond their control, and they chose to endure rather than surrender.*

She'd endured. She was still enduring.

"Tanaka-san," Gao Qiang said. "I'm going to ask you something strange, and I need you to hear me out before you answer."

She blinked. "What?"

"I'm building something in this city. A network. A — family, I guess, though that sounds insane. I'm looking for people who are tough, who've been through hell, and who aren't afraid to stand up when it matters. You just proved you're one of those people."

"I... didn't do anything. You're the one who—"

"You stayed calm. You kept your hands steady. You caught the phone. Most people would have dropped it." He held her gaze. "I'm not asking you to do anything dangerous. I'm asking if you want to be part of something where people look out for each other. Where no one stands alone in an alley at midnight."

The fluorescent bulb flickered. In its uncertain light, Tanaka Mei studied this strange Chinese man with the calm voice and the intense eyes and the weird, formal way of saying things that sounded like they'd been translated from a language she couldn't speak.

"What's in it for you?" she asked.

Good question. She was sharp.

"A sister," Gao Qiang said simply. "A sister who watches the street and tells me what she sees. That's all. And in return, you never stand alone in an alley again."

He pressed **[ RECRUIT ]**.

The golden flash. The shudder. Tanaka Mei gasped, one hand flying to her chest, and for three seconds her eyes went wide and unseeing.

Then it passed. And when she looked at Gao Qiang again, her expression was transformed. The fear was gone. The shaking had stopped. In its place was something Kenji had worn too — a quiet, deep certainty, as if a door had opened inside her and she'd stepped through into a room she'd always known existed.

"Gao Qiang-san," she said. "Big Brother."

Her voice didn't waver.

20 LP. Not enough. Not nearly enough. But the mission reward was coming — 200 LP when the 72-hour assessment window closed. He just had to hold on until then.

He walked Tanaka Mei back to the main street. Kenji was waiting where he'd been told to wait, and when he saw Tanaka Mei — and the way she positioned herself relative to Gao Qiang, the slight protective angle of her shoulders — he understood immediately.

"Another brother?" Kenji murmured.

"Sister," Gao Qiang corrected. "Tanaka Mei. Informant class."

Kenji bowed. Not to Gao Qiang this time — to Tanaka Mei. "Welcome to the family."

Tanaka Mei — who had been alone for so long she'd forgotten what it felt like to be *welcomed* anywhere — bowed back. Her eyes were bright, and she blinked rapidly, but her voice was steady.

"Thank you, Murakami-san."

They walked back to the apartment together. Three people now. A former accountant, a waitress, and a Chinese student with a system in his head and a city to conquer.

The yakitori smoke curled up from the restaurant below. Kabukicho hummed around them — ten million stories, ten million secrets, ten million people trying to survive.

Gao Qiang had three.

It was a start.
