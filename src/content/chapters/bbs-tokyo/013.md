Chapter 13 — Collections

Tuesday arrived gray and undramatic — the first whole day in two weeks that asked nothing of anyone.

The crows had the sign rail to themselves, arguing over nothing. Below the floorboards, the yakitori man hauled ice, swore at the freezer, and started the long marinade of the afternoon. No countdown hung in the corner of Gao Qiang's vision. No sedan to out-think. He checked the interface the way a man pats a pocket for keys he knows are there.

Nothing else, and nothing owed. The interface had stamped Sunday and gone still. It had never had anything to say about bars or streets or people; it watched outcomes and left the living inside them to whoever had to do it.

Kenji sat at the low table with tea and the pool notebook, dressed for work with two hours to spare, because early was how he relaxed. "Thursday, the tribute goes up at the usual bell," he said. "Same envelope, same minute. If we're boring now, we're boring to the second." He capped the pen. "Tomorrow's your class."

"I'll be on the 8:14."

"Sign the sheet." He said it the way other men say good morning.

Sato came back a little after three, in daylight, the way he had promised no one but himself. He knelt at the table and set the street down flat, in order, no face on any of it.

"The tobacco stand's shutter was half closed at noon. She used to stay open till the last train. The izakaya on Eight has paid twice since Saturday, no receipt either time. Takahashi's collecting a new fee on top of the usual. Three hundred more a week, a shutter. He calls it counting."

"Who holds the bag?"

"Mori holds. Takahashi counts."

A fee for counting, from the man doing the counting. Gao Qiang kept that where he kept it.

"Mori's asking after me," Sato went on. "Flower shop. Ticket place. The flower shop told him Yokohama." A beat. "And Hasegawa stood across from the pachinko side door this morning. Not collecting. Standing. Arm in the sling, cast to the elbow. Five days, and he's out taking the air."

"And the air he's taking is your street."

"It was mine." Sato's jaw moved once. "Somebody's."

Gao Qiang walked the position in his head, the way his father had taught him to walk a negotiation before entering the room. Hasegawa's crew had watched a stranger fold his wrist in an alley and watched his collector walk out of the story. Since then his street came up short, week after week, thirty thousand at a time — and a man in that position does the one thing left to a broken boss. He presses down. Down the street, never up the chain. Up the chain, a shortage is a confession.

But pressing down never finds a leak that stands beside the money. The hole in Hasegawa's week was inside his own bucket, and the street was being squeezed to top it up, and none of it would close the gap — because the gap had a name.

Gao Qiang had the name. He'd had it since Sato's debrief four days ago, when the crew had been laid out flat for them — who held the bag, who counted, and where a second envelope went before Menya Kuro ever saw the money. He had never said it in front of anyone. He had never written it down. Some things stay off paper until you spend them.

Not yet. Spending it now would be doing Hasegawa a favor — handing a drowning man a reason to look at his own crew instead of the water. And any answer the brotherhood gave the squeeze would carry a signature: a face, a pattern, a name someone could read later. Above them sat a man from snow country who could lose a report and could not lose a war, and everything they did on these blocks lived inside that sentence or didn't get done.

He didn't go look at the street himself, either. You don't test a wound by leaning on it, and a stranger reading shopfronts on Block Eight would be a signature too.

"The disguise costs us nothing," he said. "The street's paying it by the shutter. That stands. We don't answer it."

Sato looked at him for a moment — level, taking the weight of it onto his own shoulders — and nodded once.

Tuesday evening came down in neon, the signs waking street by street like a shift clocking in. Kojima came up the stairs with a towel around his neck and his breath still going, and didn't sit, which meant he was carrying something.

"The old man at the karaoke stairs cornered me on my cool-down. Monday, he says. Same woman came by again. Plain clothes — city kind of plain. Asked about the basement nights. Noise. Late crowds. Who rents the room down there." He worked the towel across the back of his neck. "Showed a card too fast to read it."

"Her Japanese?"

"Polite. The kind that writes things down afterward."

Sakamoto Yui. Gao Qiang kept the name behind his teeth. Community liaison, Shinjuku. She had taken the uncle story apart with three questions once, and then been shelved by a desk that hadn't looked up since. That was the part that mattered: patient. The patient ones come back with better questions.

"Community police," he said. "Liaison. She's been to this building before — first week, downstairs, when it was just the two of us. She let it go." He turned his cup a quarter and stopped himself. "Letting go is her habit. She lets things go, and she keeps them."

"So the basement's a door she's found," Sato said.

"She's found a hallway. She doesn't know which doors are ours." Gao Qiang looked at Kojima. "Nobody fights until we know what she asked and who she told."

"And the room?"

"Sits dark. Money we can replace. A basement full of people who know your face, we can't."

Kojima considered it, shoulders rolling once. "Good. I was getting tired of winning."

"How long?"

"However long. Train, run, stay off Ichibangai after dark. If she comes back, the old man doesn't know you and never did — but I hear about it that day."

"I can be boring." Kojima hung the towel on the rail. "Ask anybody."

Tuesday, ten o'clock, the stairwell delivered Mei with two bags and the cold still on her jacket.

"Out," she said, and they were.

She cooked the way she worked, no motion spent twice. Rice washed and set. Miso with wakame. Chicken thighs cut flat and seared with ginger, the fan dragging the smoke out the window slot. While the rice drank its water she reordered the oil shelf — nothing thrown away, everything moved four centimeters toward sense — and said nothing about it, then or after.

There were four bowls in the house and five of them, and she had planned for that too: three at the table, the fourth lidded with a saucer for Kenji, and herself at the pan, standing. The largest piece of chicken found Sato's bowl without comment. Nobody remarked on it, which was the point of how she'd done it.

Her report came over her shoulder, between turns of the pan.

"Monday the delivery boys went quiet. Today the greengrocer says collectors have come twice in one week. Block Nine's keeping its head down, and nobody down there says a name." She wiped her hands on the towel. "They say the sling."

"The sling," Gao Qiang said.

"That's what he is on that street now. The sling." She set the pickles down in the middle. "Names wear out. That one won't. A man who can't collect gets renamed by what he can still do."

Sato slid a cushion toward her with his foot. She looked at it.

"I stand at a counter eleven hours a day," she said. "This is variety."

"Any chance of spice in this household's future?" Kojima said.

"There is correct," Mei said, "and there is incorrect. Eat."

They ate. Sato cleared the bowls without being asked and Kojima washed up after, sleeves pushed high, handling the dishes the way he handled laundry — carefully, like things someone owned on purpose. Mei stood at the counter and watched the three of them under the kitchen light, and if there was a word for what that was, she didn't offer it and neither did anyone else.

At eleven she gathered her coat. At the door she looked back once at the shelf she'd reordered and said nothing about it, which was how she said most things.

"Fourth bowl's lidded," she said, going down the stairs. "He eats it warm or not at all."

---

Tuesday, quarter past eleven, Kenji took his coat off before he knelt — the first time. The shape of things no longer needed guarding while it set.

"The Goose kept time," he said. "The back-room key turned once, at nine. Bottles at ten, till counted at eleven exactly. He didn't speak tonight. Not four words. None." He paused. "The whiskey bottle didn't move either. He's learning what the arrangement is."

The saved bowl was still warm under the saucer. He ate all of it, set the chopsticks down aligned, and nodded once at the kitchen.

"Tell her it was the correct kind."

Then the short council, voices low, futons half rolled out around it. Gao Qiang laid it flat: two pressures on the same block of street, one answer. Nothing grows past the tea. The venue stays dark, Sato stays in daylight, the street gets watched and not touched.

"If the squeeze reaches the Goose, or our door, that's different," he said. "Until then it's Hasegawa drowning in his own bucket, and we're not obliged to tell him where the hole is."

"Thursday is the first test of boring," Kenji said. "The bell. I'll be standing there at the minute." He opened the ledger and wrote while he talked, because for Kenji saying a thing and entering it were the same act. "Month's end gives us two appointments. The landlord takes ninety from the pool — we're at one seventy-six and a half, and Saturday my ninety goes in. The envelope never touches this pool at all. It leaves the bar's margin and goes straight to the tea."

"Month's end," Sato said, from his corner. "I know the door now."

That was the whole meeting. Kojima folded out the futons in their rows, and the apartment dimmed by degrees, window last.

Gao Qiang stood at it a moment. Somewhere south, the sling was learning to be a rumor. The hole in Hasegawa's week would go on refusing to close, and the street would go on paying for it shutter by shutter, and a day would come when the name in Gao Qiang's head was worth spending.

Not this week.

Three appointments stood ahead, all of them ordinary: tomorrow the 8:14, a sign-in sheet, and Park Soo-jin, who counted, counting him there; Friday, one hour, the folder at the bottom of his bag; month's end, the tea.

Ordinary was the work now. It kept hours.

He turned off the light while the last trains were still dragging their glow up the street, and in the morning he would stand in a seminar room in Hiyoshi and be counted present — the easiest thing on the list, and the only one he dared not miss.
