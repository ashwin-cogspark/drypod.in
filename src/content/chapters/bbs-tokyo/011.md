Chapter 11 — The Last Card

Sunday arrived the way a collection date arrives for a debtor: no announcement, just the day itself, sitting on the doorstep.

Gao Qiang woke at six to a silence that had texture. No pachinko chirp yet, no delivery engines idling in the alleys. Under the floorboards, the yakitori restaurant had begun its prep — water running, the grill scraped clean, the first smoke of the morning climbing the shaft and seeping up into the apartment with the smell of charcoal and chicken fat.

It mixed with the ghost of yesterday's tea. He let it.

He had slept three hours. His notebook lay open where he'd left it, three columns inked in the dark: what they knew, what they guessed, what they'd lost. The third column had one entry, circled twice.

Kenji was awake first, kneeling at the low table with his back straight, the way he knelt for everything.

Kojima's breathing still came from the far corner, deep and even, the sleep of a body that healed on schedule. By the door, Sato's futon was folded. The boy himself sat in the window corner, knees up, phone face-down beside his knee.

Same phone. Same position as last night.

Nobody commented on it.

The kettle went on. Outside, the first shutter of the day clapped open on the shopping street. A garbage truck worked the block with sighing brakes, and somewhere below, a wet sidewalk was being squeegeed toward the storm drain.

Sunday morning in Kabukicho was the neighborhood with its stage makeup off — one honest hour before the lights came back on.

---

Kenji's first act of the day, before greeting, before sitting properly, was to open the pool ledger.

"Saturday's pay," he said. "I didn't log it last night."

He wrote in small, even figures. *Payday, Saturday: +90,000.*

Then he carried the running total forward, drew the line under it, and spoke the arithmetic the way an auditor speaks numbers he refuses to let anyone round.

"Eighty-six thousand five hundred before the envelope. Plus ninety thousand. One hundred seventy-six thousand five hundred yen." The pen stopped. "That's real. That's what we have."

"You logged it now because you think we might need to know the number exactly."

Kenji closed the notebook. "I logged it because I was sloppy. Same thing, maybe."

Gao Qiang turned his own notebook toward him, to the page that had stopped being useful around three in the morning.

"The sedan came Saturday night," Kenji said quietly. "Off-schedule. Mei called before her shift — six-forty. She was watching from the counter."

"The driver?"

"In through the back. Forty minutes. Nakamura walked him out bowing." Kenji's hands stayed flat on the table. "Not the bow you give a delivery man."

"The bow you give someone you've asked a favor of."

"Yes."

Outside, a pachinko parlor ran its morning test-chirp, a bright mechanical rattle through the closed window.

"So let's say it plainly," Gao Qiang said. "You pitched him the skim, the clean books, the percentage. He asked how many people knew. Names."

"I gave him two. You and me. I thought—" Kenji stopped. Started again. "I thought a man who'd stolen from the yakuza for two years would recognize an exit when it walked in wearing a tie."

"He did recognize it. He recognized it as currency." Gao Qiang tapped the circled entry. "We walked into his bar and handed him the only thing he's ever needed to buy his way back into good standing: us."

"The report isn't sitting in his back room. It went up with the sedan. Every hour it moves another layer, and every layer it passes, our names get harder to pull back."

The block hung in the corner of his vision like a stamp on an unpaid invoice. Gao Qiang had checked it four times since waking, hoping the deadline would change. It did not change.

The system tracked missions and brothers and the number in his chest that meant Sato would not leave him. It did not track a sixty-year-old bar owner picking up a telephone.

That was the lesson, and he'd paid for it.

Kojima came back from the conbini at nine with onigiri and a bruised-looking energy about him.

He set the bag down the way he'd set everything down since the shoulder healed — deliberately, both hands, testing the joint — then read the table, read their faces, and didn't sit.

The apartment seemed to contract around him. He filled the genkan the way a batter fills a box, and chose to stay in it.

"How bad," he said.

"Nakamura sold us," Kenji said. "The report's moving up the tribute chain. Deadline's tonight."

"Options."

"Conventional ones first." Gao Qiang counted them off the way he'd count inventory. "We go back to Nakamura and threaten him. He's already spent us — there's nothing left to threaten with."

"We go over his head ourselves — we don't exist to anyone up there except as a name in a report, and the men who read those reports solve problems with pipe."

"We walk away — the deadline dies and the leverage with it, and the names in that report eventually reach someone curious."

"Kill the report at the top," Kojima said. It wasn't a suggestion. It was him checking whether it was on the list.

"We don't know who the top is. We know the sedan driver. We know he reports to someone. That's the whole map."

Silence.

Sato had been in the corner since Kenji's first sentence, kneeling by the window with the phone still face-down beside his knee, and he had not said a word. That was normal for Sato. What was not normal was the phone.

He kept glancing at it the way a man looks at a letter he's carried too long unopened.

---

Mei arrived at nine-twenty, hair still damp, already in her Ramen Ya undershirt with an hour and a half to burn before her shift.

She came in without knocking and stood in the genkan, and the street came in with her — grease smoke, ticket-rail chatter, the particular tiredness of a person who has been awake among other people's conversations for an hour.

That was her mornings now. The counter had taught her to drink the neighborhood: delivery boys, part-timers, hostesses on their way home, salarymen confessing half-truths into their soup. By nine she usually knew more about Kabukicho's mood than the men who owned it.

"Two things," she said. "Fast."

"One — Nakamura closed the back room to staff this morning. Matsuda tried to get his coat out of there and got told to come back Tuesday. Nakamura never closes that room."

"Cleaning up paper," Kenji said.

"Or having a meeting in it. Two—" Mei's voice dropped, not for drama, just habit. "A woman came into the shop last night. Plain clothes, plain questions. Asked Marui-chan about the karaoke basement. The fights."

Gao Qiang set his pen down. "Which basement."

"The one you're thinking of. Marui said she asked like she already knew the answers. Polite. Wrote nothing down."

"Sakamoto," Gao Qiang said.

"You know her?"

"Liaison officer. Sharp. She'll be back."

He filed it — the way he filed rent, the way he filed the visa sessions stacking against him at Keio — in the drawer of problems that could not be solved this morning.

"Mei. Anything else?"

"The sedan driver's name. One of the bartenders heard Nakamura call him Endo. That's all."

She left for her shift. Kojima ate a rice ball standing up and said, "So we're out of moves," and it wasn't a question, and Gao Qiang didn't insult him by pretending otherwise.

---

Gao Qiang dealt with the next problem on its own terms, because it had a time attached.

"Kenji. Your shift. Five o'clock."

Kenji's jaw moved. "I know."

"You'd be walking into the bar of the man who sold us, to pour whiskey for strangers, and smile at him."

"I understand what it is." A pause. "It's also six hours of wages and a view of the back-room door. If Nakamura keeps me on the schedule, he's decided I'm more useful in place than in a report. If he cuts the schedule—" Kenji straightened his collar with two fingers, an old corporate reflex. "Then we learn something too."

"It's a risk."

"Everything today is a risk. This one pays three thousand yen an hour, which is at least precise."

It was almost funny. Gao Qiang let himself hold that for one breath, then let it go.

The room had gone quiet again, and in the quiet Sato picked up his phone, turned it over, and put it back down without waking the screen. Third time. Kojima saw it. Kenji saw it.

Nobody said anything, because Sato's silences were load-bearing and you didn't pull things out of them.

Gao Qiang decided to.

"Sato. You've been sitting on something since yesterday."

Sato's shoulders didn't move, but the stillness changed quality. "Since longer than yesterday," he said.

"Take the time you need."

"You don't have time. That's the problem." Sato looked at the phone. Then at Gao Qiang. "You know where I'm from."

"Yuzawa. Akita."

"Snow country. A town where everyone knows everyone, and everyone knows the orphanage kids especially." His voice stayed level, spare, each sentence laid down like a plank across something deep. "There was a boy in my neighborhood. Older than me."

"Ten years older — he was seventeen when he left for Tokyo, I was seven. In a town that size, a kid who leaves for Tokyo and comes back once a year in a good coat is a legend. He used to buy shaved ice for all of us in summer. The orphanage kids. Me."

The phone stayed face-down. He talked to the window instead.

"When I aged out and came down here with a bag and a train ticket, he was the only name I had. He'd made something of himself — collections, an office, a future. He made a call and got me a spot. Vouched for me with his own name. That's how I ended up under Hasegawa."

"Name," Kenji said softly.

"Hayashi. Hayashi Makoto." Sato let it sit. "He's not a collector anymore. He runs the tribute zone. All of Kabukicho's collections pass through his office. The sedan reports to him. Endo reports to him."

Kojima put down the onigiri.

"Every report Nakamura files," Sato said, "lands on Hayashi's desk before it goes anywhere near Kuroda's people. That's the structure. I've sat in that office. I've poured coffee in that office."

Gao Qiang kept his voice as flat as the table. "Why didn't you say this before?"

"Because I owe him." Sato's hands closed, opened. "He got me in. When I left Hasegawa's crew — when you took me in — I made his vouching worthless. If I show up now asking for a favor, I'm telling him the kid from Yuzawa spat on his name and needs it saved anyway."

He stopped. Looked at the phone again. "That's my shame. Not yours. But you can't have it and my silence both."

The clock in the corner of Gao Qiang's vision ticked its way under twelve hours.

"Would he help?" Gao Qiang asked. "Honestly."

"Honestly — I don't know. He's not sentimental. He's fair, in his way, and he protects his zone, and a report about outsiders with ledgers makes his zone look penetrated. That's a problem on his desk whether I exist or not."

Sato paused. "But he won't die for me. And he won't stick his neck past his own authority. Anyone who goes to him should know that going in."

---

They worked it like an accounting problem, because that was what it was.

The report had to stop at Hayashi's level or not at all. Above Hayashi, the names became Kuroda's problem, and Kuroda's problems got solved with pipe. Hayashi was the last door, and the door only opened for one person.

"Me," Sato said. "It has to be me. He doesn't know your face, Gao Qiang. He knows mine, and he knows what my face costs him. If a stranger comes with this story, it's a threat. If I come, it's a debt."

"It's also you walking into the man whose reputation you spent," Kenji said. "Alone."

"I've been alone in worse rooms."

Gao Qiang looked at him — nineteen, a year and a half out of foster care, sitting straight-backed with his shame on the table between them, offering it up like it was money.

There was a number in Gao Qiang's chest for Sato and it had never wavered, and he understood, not for the first time, that the system could not have bought this. This, Sato was choosing.

Gao Qiang laid the terms out one by one, the way he laid out a plan, and inside his head each one had a weight.

"Then we set the terms before you go. Three things. You don't mention the brotherhood — no names, no apartment, no Mei, no Kojima, no Kenji's ledger. If Hayashi helps, he helps for his own reasons: his zone, his report, his debt. We stay invisible. That's not caution, that's survival — if Kuroda's people learn we exist, everything we've built ends in a night."

"Second," Kenji said, "we don't ask him to destroy the report. Too big. We ask him to sit on it, hold it, decide it goes nowhere. A supervisor can lose paperwork for a week. A supervisor can't burn it."

"Third." Gao Qiang waited until Sato met his eyes. "You tell him the truth about what you are now. Not the system — you. A man who works for people who don't throw him away. Hayashi got you into Hasegawa's crew, and that crew was going to get you killed. You don't say it like an accusation. You say it so he knows what he's weighing."

Sato nodded slowly. "And if he says no?"

"Then he says no, and you come home, and we spend tomorrow learning to live smaller." Gao Qiang closed the notebook. "You don't beg. You're not the kid from Yuzawa anymore."

Kojima cracked his knuckles, one hand, quiet. "When?"

"Tonight, after Hayashi's office settles. Sato knows the hour better than we do."

"After nine," Sato said. "Endo's runs are done by eight. He takes tea at nine. He always took tea at nine."

Of course he did. Some things a man remembers about the person who saved him.

---

The afternoon went by at the speed afternoon goes when there is nothing in it but waiting.

Kenji stood, checked his watch, and reached for his work shirt at four. He ironed it with the attention he gave a ledger line — collar, cuffs, the crease of the sleeve — and hung it on the rail.

For a moment Gao Qiang watched him choose the face he would wear at five o'clock: the dependable, slightly dull accountant who had made one clumsy pitch and let it die on a Saturday. Twenty-five years of boards and audits had taught him to wear competence like a coat, and dullness over it like a second coat.

At the door, Kenji paused.

"If the schedule holds, I'll be behind the counter until eleven. If anything moves at the back-room door, you'll hear from me before it finishes moving."

"Only if it's safe to send."

"It will be safe to send. I'm a man who counts bottles." A beat. "That's all I've ever been, as far as that bar knows."

He went down the stairs with the ironed shirt in a garment bag, and the apartment settled into its Sunday rhythms around the wait.

Mei was at her counter across the neighborhood, absorbing whatever drifted in with the broth steam. Kojima did his breathing work on the roof, came down, cleaned the teacups without being asked, and then sat where he could see both the door and Sato.

Sato sat by the window most of the afternoon with the phone in his lap, not open, not off. Once, Gao Qiang nearly said something. He didn't. Some calls couldn't be made for someone.

The light went from white to gold to the particular amber Kabukicho saves for its evenings, when the neon starts losing its argument with the sky.

Sato changed into the shirt Kenji had left for him on the rail — pressed, neutral, anonymous — and washed his face for a long time at the sink.

"Use the roof if you want quiet," Gao Qiang said. "No street noise up there."

Sato shook his head. "If I go up early, I'll talk myself out of it by eight. We go at eight-forty. Walk the long way. I want my legs under me before I'm standing at his door."

Kojima stood, rolled his shoulders once, and took his jacket off the hook. "Edge of the zone," he said. "I'll wait where waiting looks like nothing."

They went down the stairs together, the fighter and the boy from Yuzawa, and the stairwell door swung shut behind them.

The apartment was suddenly very quiet in the way of rooms that have said what they had to say.

Gao Qiang sat at the low table with both notebooks open — Kenji's pool ledger and his own three columns — and did the only useful thing left: nothing, deliberately, like a man who has placed his piece and waits for the board to answer.

Through the kitchen window, the sky over the rooftops was flat and darkening. Somewhere south, Mei's counter was entering the last hour of the shift.

Somewhere west, Kenji was counting bottles in the bar of the man who had sold them, wearing dullness like a coat.

And somewhere north of the neon, a boy who used to get shaved ice in summer was walking toward an office where tea was taken at nine, carrying a debt thirteen months old and a story that had to be true.

"Whatever he decides," Sato had said at the door, "it's mine to carry in there."

"I know," Gao Qiang had said. "That's why it's you."
