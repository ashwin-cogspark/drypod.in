Chapter 17 — The Window

Sunday evening, and the six-o'clock trade was waking up behind him as Murakami Kenji took the long way to The Golden Goose — down the alley behind the print shop, at a walker's pace.

The shutter stood half down. In the street window, taped where a rack of business cards used to sit, a letting sheet: white paper, a corporate logo, a management office with an address across the river. Nobody from the blocks. A name that had never needed a face here.

At the sheet's corner, printed small and square: sixty-five thousand a month.

He read it the way he read everything — as terms. Sixty-five in, monthly, against a pool holding two hundred sixty-six thousand five hundred. Ninety of that owed on the apartment at month's end. The sheet named no deposit. The deposit would come when he asked, and whatever it was, it had to fit inside what remained after the rent — or the numbers did not close.

The alley door stood shut and ran through to the service street the way Sato had said it did. Above it, the room's window sat dark and unremarkable, one window among many on the block. At the alley's mouth the noodle cart was setting up for the night trade, tank lit, steam lifting off the first stock. The man kept his back to the street and his face to the alley door. Years he must have watched it. He asked nothing.

Kenji asked nothing either. No question, no name, nothing signed. He checked his watch against his shift and walked on to the Goose, and in his coat the report for the table stayed folded at one line.

---

Monday, noon. Ichibangai in daylight looked smaller and more honest than the street he walked at night. Sato Kenjiro went up it unmarked, hands empty, pace even.

The collection truck stood at its corner. The difference was the man beside it — not the one who used to stand there, but Endo himself, the sedan driver out of the car and working the count. Shutter to shutter, metronome-exact. No counting fee called. No second pass. The takes went into the car's bag, the bag went into the car, and the car moved on.

Okada's corner was unchained. Padlock off, shutters up, the man himself back behind his news rack with the sorting hand splinted and resting on a folded towel, out of the work. He counted bundles with his good hand, one at a time, and the street counted him the way it counted everyone, and nobody said one word to him about any of it. That was the mercy here: to be nobody special, on a street that forgave nothing.

At the pachinko hall's side door, Hasegawa had moved his stool. He sat with the cast in its sling, watching a count he no longer ran. What remained of the crew stood at the wall with nothing in their hands — short one man, and the street did not say his name, and neither did Sato.

The bounty had died over the weekend without a word said, no paper on any wall. The street read Monday for itself: the zone, tidying its own blocks.

Sato bought nothing. Eyes crossed him and went past, through him. He walked home the long way, past the shrine, carrying the day's confirmation. Everything he had been told would be true today was true — to the shutter, and to the sling.

---

Monday, quarter past ten at night, the low table. Gao Qiang had signed the morning sheet at Keio; the count still stood at three. He poured for five.

Tanaka Mei came in off shift with her coat still on and gave the nephew's report standing, in order.

"Three things. At nine this morning the nephew stood at the koban window himself and signed the family's answer into the document, his own hand. Two — the lease on the basement sits with the management company. Letters to it come back sealed. The wall is the same wall it has always been. Three — in the signed paper, the figure on the dark room climbed. What the family declared is higher than the fights ever paid. The dark room is dressed up as the building's best tenant." A breath. "The karaoke club's name is nowhere in the answer. The club is intact."

"Two halves," Gao Qiang said, setting them side by side for the table. "The name is dead. A wall is where a trail ends; nobody comes over it from our side. The paper is alive. Signed means it travels, and a number that high on a basement travels with it. Anyone who reads that file keeps reading, and readers move on their own clocks." He let it sit. "The basement is finished as a venue. We go off-block."

Sato gave his read first, spare as always. "Endo ran it. Exact, one pass, no fee. Okada's unchained, back at his rack, hand splinted, counted like the rest. Crew's short one. The street thinks the zone cleaned its own house."

Kojima nodded once.

Kenji unfolded the one line from his coat and gave it to the table in his own order: the sheet in the window under a management name from across the river; sixty-five a month; the alley door running through to the service street; the room's window dark and forgettable; the noodle cart facing the door after six. "The deposit isn't on the sheet."

"Then we relight," Gao Qiang said. "Small first card. No book the first night — nobody bets, nobody owes. The door opens when the cart goes home and not before. And the deposit has to be a number this pool can meet with the apartment rent still standing beside it at month's end."

"I call at nine tomorrow," Kenji said. "Terms plain. Nothing signed until this table has heard them."

At the word call, Kojima took a folded paper from inside his jacket and set it face down next to his cup — hospital letterhead showing along one fold, the million and a half it named turned to the wood. He poured more tea and said nothing. Nobody said anything. Nobody had to.

Mei looked at the table — the ruling her one word had started, back when her verdict was Dark — and reached for her coat. "Then it's moving," she said, and was gone for the last train.

Four at the table. The week set itself in a row: the call at nine, the signing only if the terms came back plain, the card small and late. On the shelf above the tatami the Roppongi page sat folded where it had sat all week, untabled, his alone. Its hour came on Fridays, in daylight. Four days out.

Gao Qiang let the quiet hold. Nine o'clock would come early.
