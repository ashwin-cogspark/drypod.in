Chapter 17 — The Window

Sunday ran on the blocks' slow hours. Shutters went up past ten, the pachinko halls breathed last night's smoke out into the street, and the yakitori counter under the apartment cooked for strays and students over half its charcoal banked.

In the apartment, the week sat where it had been set down. Murakami Kenji ironed his shirt for the Goose with the pocket book still folded in his jacket, unopened. Sato Kenjiro had gone out at seven and come back with his hair wet and ate rice standing at the counter, the way he ate everything, like it might be taken from him. Kojima Haruto put the kettle on and didn't talk about Monday, which weighed the same as talking about it.

Gao Qiang let the day stay where it was. You don't adjust a position the day before it moves on its own. He walked the blocks once in the afternoon, unhurried, a student with a can of coffee. Okada's shop stood lit and open and empty, the old man sorting cartridges left-handed for nobody. Hasegawa sat on his stool under the awning facing the street, and it looked like the last full day of it anyone would get.

He ate early at Mei's counter, through the steam. The grandson from the karaoke building sat at the far end with a coffee he was holding instead of drinking, both hands around the cup, the hands doing the work the cup should have. The weekend the man had agreed to ran out at midnight, and everyone in the room could feel it running.

"He asked again what time it opens," Mei said, setting down water he hadn't ordered. "Nine, I told him. He nodded like I'd quoted him a price."

After dark, Kojima found him on the rooftop with the corner burning low below them — the game shop's light, the pachinko sign's buzz, a taxi turning off the rank. Kojima rolled his healed shoulder once, testing it the way a man tests a stair rail.

"Ring rust," he said. "Give me five weeks and a body to practice on."

"Tomorrow Kenji puts his eyes on the room," Gao Qiang said. "Then we call the door."

Kojima looked at the blocks instead of answering. He pressed his hand flat against the left breast of his jacket, where the receipt lived, and took it away, and said nothing at all.

Monday would run three changes at once — a window at nine, a count at noon, a door in the afternoon — and none of the three would wait on the others. Gao Qiang let the day stand and went down to make tea.

---

Monday, nine o'clock, the koban was glass on three sides with a kettle inside it and one sergeant at the desk. Gao Qiang took the bench across the street with a hot can of coffee and no newspaper, because a man reading nothing is furniture and a man reading something is a witness.

The taxi rank two doors down was quiet, and every driver in it was facing the same glass.

The grandson came early. He stood through two changes of the crossing light with his hands in his pockets, and when the light changed a third time he crossed. At the door of the koban he stopped, and before he had lifted a hand the sergeant slid it open for him, and that was the moment the number stopped belonging to the street.

Gao Qiang drank his coffee and thought it through, because thinking it through was all the bench left him. A rumor was the crowd's property — it grew because nobody owned it, forty thousand yen because four thousand yen felt small, a golf club because a beating needed a shape. A signature made it official. The complaint would go to a ward office or a desk, and one desk in Shinjuku had spent the spring learning to read this street's paper.

None of it pointed at the five of them. The complaint had a club in it and a corner under it, and the corner wore other men's names. That was the arrangement holding. But whatever attention came to these blocks from now on came from the official side, and anything they started of their own would have to happen somewhere else.

He didn't wait for the grandson to come out. The street would carry that by lunch, with improvements. He dropped the can in the bin and walked to the station the long way.

---

Monday noon, the sedan came into the back alley behind Menya Kuro at twelve exactly, and the corner changed hands without moving an inch.

From the rooftop, Gao Qiang stood at the rail with Sato beside him, two sets of eyes on one street, only one of them free. Endo got out with a hard case and set it on the trunk lid. He counted the way he did crosswords — in ink, in order, nothing twice. The pachinko runners and the two who worked the game streets lined up with envelopes. Mori held the bag open with the same face he'd held it closed with on Thursday.

Okada came last, left-handed, and Endo counted him to the yen like everyone else. That was the change in full: nothing on this corner was personal anymore, including the grudge.

The old count had run on Hasegawa's mood — afternoons, sometimes skipped, twice in a day when a story needed settling. The new one ran on the hour. By the second envelope the shutter men were timing their deliveries around it, and a taxi driver asked the rank what time it was without meaning the train. The street had traded a man for a clock, and clocks don't take sides. They just never stop.

Hasegawa sat under the awning with a sports paper he wasn't reading. At some point that morning the stool had been turned to face the pachinko hall. The corner kept his name; the count kept a schedule; and somewhere above the sedan, a report got shorter and a number got cleaner, which was all anyone up there wanted from these blocks.

"Endo counts at noon," Sato said, like a man reading a sign aloud. "Mori holds the bag. Nobody says anything to anybody."

"What do you carry to Hayashi?"

"Nothing this week." Sato watched the trunk lid close. "He wants boring. Boring is no report." His shoulders came down half an inch, the way they did when he put a thing somewhere he couldn't reach, and then he let it go.

---

Monday, two o'clock, two stops north, the train let them out like strangers. Kenji wore the suit he wore for banks and carried a rental folder, and looked like what the building was owed: a man with a form. Gao Qiang took the far mouth of the alley at a walk with no purpose in it.

The street was iron shutters and small trades. The print shop ran warm paper and solvent smell into the alley, its roller up, two men at the machines. The unit behind it met the world through one steel door, and the alley ran through the block, mouth to mouth — two streets, and neither exit the one you came in by. They crossed once in the middle, going opposite ways, and met again three streets over at a bank of vending machines.

"Separate commercial meter," Kenji said, the folder squared under his arm. "The room holds what the stair held, tighter. Steel door, two ways out, no stairs to trap anyone in. Landlord is a management company — the letting sheet gives a phone number, not a name. You get procedure. Deposit in writing, contract, a fax. No face to owe and no face watching. It cuts both edges."

"The street?"

"Quiet. Working blocks, closed by eight. One problem." He tipped his head back the way they'd come. "A noodle cart at the alley mouth from six. He watches that alley for everyone, which means for us too. After six, nothing moves out that door he doesn't see. And the stair is done regardless — the ward men walked it, there's a lease question on that basement with a stamp on it now. If we relight, it's this room or nothing."

He checked his watch the way other men check wounds and left for the Goose.

Monday night, after eleven, the kitchen filled. Kenji came in off his shift; Mei arrived after closing with the night's leftovers and stacked them on their lids before she said anything.

"He signed," she said. "Twenty minutes, and the sergeant walked him to the door. By dinner the rank had him paid to do it, and the forty-thousand figure went to sixty on the way over. The club stayed in." She set out containers. "Nobody I've ever served has mentioned that alley up north. That's rarer than you'd think."

Kojima reached into his jacket and laid the clinic receipt on the table, face down. He put his hand flat on it for a second like it was warm.

"One million five," he said. "That's the number on the clinic receipt. Standing still is the most expensive thing I do. It's the only thing I've been doing."

Sato spoke with his eyes on the receipt. "The room's off-block. Nobody from the corner works two stops north. I'd have heard."

Gao Qiang looked down the table and made the call the way he'd been weighing it since the vending machines.

"We relight. One card, small — faces we know, no book the first night, and the cart's gone home before the door opens. If the room holds quiet through one night, the second card grows. Kenji?"

"I'll call the number at nine, when offices open. We don't move until the deposit is a number we can meet." Kenji took out the pocket book and, this time, opened it.

Kojima turned the receipt over, looked at it once, and put it away. "Five weeks to work it off," he said. "I'll be ready when the card's set."

Later, with the light down to the one over the stove, Gao Qiang let the week run itself out in front of him: a phone call Tuesday, the chair at Keio on Wednesday and its sign-in sheet with Soo-jin counting heads, the sedan Thursday, Friday's hour with a door in it, the rent due Saturday — and the envelope after, on its own trip, alone.

He touched the inside pocket once, flat, and took nothing out. The fourth-floor door had kept whatever it held for two summers. Four more days wouldn't kill anything.

He put out the light. The old stair was dead paper, the new room was a phone call, and for the first time in weeks the schedule was theirs to set.
