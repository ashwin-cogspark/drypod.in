Chapter 14 — The Stamp

Wednesday came in on the 8:14, the gray burning off to thin sun by Hiyoshi. Ogawa took the stair seat beside him and delivered a ruling on the professor's new slide deck — "criminally dense, and I say that as a friend of density" — and Gao Qiang let it pass through him like station noise.

The Group 7 room smelled of whiteboard marker and wet umbrellas. Park Soo-jin had the sign-in sheet squared to the table's edge beside her notes, and when the hour began she dealt it down the row without a word. He took the pen when it reached him and wrote his name in the box, strokes even, date correct, and passed it on. The count stayed at three.

The session ran long on hedging models. Ogawa produced four pages of notes he would never read again. At the bottom of Gao Qiang's bag, the folder kept its Friday hour, untouched.

After, while the room emptied, Soo-jin squared the sheet into her binder and spoke at the volume she used for logistics.

"The office asks me for the number. It stands at three." She closed the binder. "One more and I write letters."

"Understood," he said, and gave nothing. Her eyes rested on him a moment, as if measuring whether anything had moved behind his face. Nothing had. She nodded and went to her next thing, and Ogawa, arriving late at the door with his bag half-zipped, seemed not to notice any of it.

He walked to the station turning the shape of it over. A tripwire stated once, in order, no second warning — that was her version of fairness. And this was the one front where nothing he carried could help him. No ability read an attendance sheet. No plan signed a name in a box. Presence or absence, and presence he could do.

---

Wednesday night, ten-fifteen, Mei came up the stairs with the cold still on her jacket and a lidded container of staff ramen for the table. She set the container down and did not unbutton the coat.

"Everyone sits," she said. "This came to my counter tonight."

She gave it in order, the way she gave everything. The karaoke building owner's nephew — a Ramen Ya regular, careless about pickles — had been at her counter. A koban officer had walked the street today. Clipboard. Not conversation — a round. Shopkeepers asked to initial that they had been asked.

"Initialed what?" Kojima said.

"That it happened." Her hands stayed flat on the table. "And the ward office has asked his uncle for the building's drawings. They promised a visit after. To look at the basement."

Sato turned his cup a quarter turn and left it.

"So she stopped asking," Kojima said, "and started filing."

Gao Qiang set the pieces in a row and moved each one forward. Plainclothes questions were weather; they ended with the shift. Initials on a clipboard were a document, and documents outlasted shifts — they got filed, and files got pulled. A drawings request was aim, not curiosity: the paper was about the door itself now, not the noise on the stairs. And paper like that did not write itself. The desk that had shelved Sakamoto had looked up. Somewhere in a tray, her folder carried a stamp.

"Her questions had no weight before," he said. "Now they do. Initialed, dated, filed. The drawings mean the basement is the subject of a file, not a complaint. Someone above the koban is reading it."

"How fast is paper?" Sato said.

"Slower than people. Longer-lived than people." He looked at the container, still lidded, going cold. "We decide with a full table. Kenji's home at quarter past."

Kojima got bowls without being asked. Mei finally took off the coat.

---

Wednesday, quarter past eleven, Kenji came in wearing the Goose's quiet — the key had turned once, the till counted out at eleven exactly. They gave him the nephew in order: the round, the initials, the drawings, the visit promised after. He listened standing, coat over his arm, and hung it up before he spoke.

"A drawings request opens a file number," he said. "A file number carries a schedule. Paper is patient, but it is not stationary." He knelt at the low table. "So. The venue."

"Question's simple," Gao Qiang said. "How long it sits dark, and what dark costs. All of it, on the table."

Kojima put his hands flat, the way he did before stating weight. "Then here's the weight. Dark means no fights. No fights means no fight money. I've got a million and a half in hospital debt, and none of it cares what the ward office is doing." He said it once, flat, no lean into it. "I can train in the dark. I'm saying what it costs."

"Noted and entered," Kenji said, and his pen moved.

Sato spoke from his corner. "There's a print shop a block off Ichibangai. I collected from them. Before." Nobody made him say before what. "There's a room above it. Door faces the alley. You can walk it at noon, and nobody watches an alley at noon. If the door has to move, that one moves."

"Daylight only," Gao Qiang said. "And only if it comes to that."

Mei had been quiet through the weighing, hands around her bowl. She set it down.

"Dark," she said.

One word, and it settled the room the way her shelving did — nothing moved, everything held.

Gao Qiang gave it edges. "Then it's decided. Dark through the weekend. Monday we review what the paper's done — where the visit stands, what the ward's answered. If it's gone nowhere, the shop is Sato's to walk, noon, no earlier. Not before." He looked around the table. "Kojima off Ichibangai after dark, minimum through the weekend. Sato in daylight, always. The street keeps paying by the shutter, and we stay off the paper."

Kenji opened the pool notebook to a clean line and wrote while he spoke, because for him saying a thing and entering it were one act. "Thursday. Tribute at the usual bell. Same walk, same minute, boring to the second — that is tomorrow's entire job." The pen stopped. "Monday I'll want the ward's answer in writing, or the absence of one. Absences tell you things too."

Futons came out in their rows. Mei gathered her coat from the hook that had become hers without anyone deciding it, and went down the stairs.

Gao Qiang stood at the window while the apartment dimmed by degrees. The stamp was quiet — that was its whole trick. It took a patient woman's questions and gave them a schedule. So they would answer patience with patience, set against a dark basement and a million and a half, and Monday would say what the waiting was worth.

Under everything, tomorrow's bell ticked. It would ring, and they would be boring to the second, and the weekend would stay dark on purpose — which was work now, the ordinary kind, the kind that kept hours.
