Chapter 14 — The Stamp

Wednesday. The 8:14 out of Shinjuku smelled of dry wool and canned coffee, a schoolbag pressed against his knees, and Gao Qiang spent the first three stations laying the day out like a board: Kenji home until his five o'clock shift, Mei behind her counter from eleven, Kojima loose in the city with his hands and nothing to point them at, Sato somewhere walking in daylight. Tomorrow the envelope went up at the same minute it had gone up last Thursday, and the whole art of tomorrow was to be unremarkable at exactly the right second.

An hour of rail for one hour of chair. That was the price of the sheet, and nobody else could pay it for him.

Hiyoshi came up out of the ground into thin, clean light, students moving in one slow current toward the gates. On the third floor of the policy building, Group 7 had pushed its chairs into the same broken horseshoe as every week. Park Soo-jin sat at the head of it with her clipboard.

"Sign," she said, without looking up.

He signed. Her pen moved down the names, making small exact marks, and at the bottom she said, "All present," in the tone of a woman confirming a delivery has arrived intact. Monday she had said *Three* the way you drop a coin into a well. Today her shoulders came down half an inch. Then, quieter, with no tone at all: "Three misses this term. One more and I write letters nobody reads."

"Noted," he said.

Ogawa Ryo leaned across the horseshoe and set a canned coffee on his desk, cold from the machine. "You have the face of a man who commutes from a war," he said. "Drink this and tell me nothing."

Gao Qiang drank it and told him nothing, which Ogawa appeared to find restful.

The professor was on land readjustment again — who holds the paper when a block changes hands, how long a former owner keeps a claim nobody intends to honor. Gao Qiang answered one question, cleanly, because it was a question about positions and he had lately been tutored in that subject by professionals. The professor looked pleasantly surprised. Park Soo-jin wrote something short.

On the ride back, between stations, he let the interface surface once.

Wednesday, past three, Kabukicho idled. Shutters up at the ironmonger, down at the bars; pachinko doors propped open, the halls' electronic chirp spilling onto the sidewalk at half volume, the first losers of the day blinking in the daylight. On the corner by the game shop, Hasegawa's sling held court with two men, laughing louder than a man with one working arm should.

At the Ramen Ya the burner hissed and the steam carried pork bone and scorched ginger. Mei worked the counter with her economy of motion, and she talked over her shoulder between tasks, the way she delivered everything — moving.

"Two uniforms this morning. Out of the koban, ten o'clock, walking slow. One had a clipboard." She knuckled the broth pot down into its well. "They stopped in front of the karaoke building. One of them looked at the basement stair long enough to write something down. Then they asked the tobacco woman whether the street gets loud after ten."

"Routine," Gao Qiang said.

"That's the word they used." One pass of the cloth across the counter. "Marui heard from a regular that somebody asked the upstairs tenant about noise, too. Two doors, one question."

He ate. Uniforms at midnight were weather; the street didn't even lift its head. Uniforms at ten in the morning with a clipboard were a letter being walked by hand, and the writing, not the baton, was the part the street should have feared. When he finished, Mei set down a glass of water he hadn't ordered, and that was the whole of her opinion.

---

Wednesday, when the neon came on and the street changed shifts, he came back for a second bowl he didn't want, to stand at her counter while the nephew told his story to the room.

"The nephew drinks here most weeks," Mei said, wringing a towel. "His aunt owns the karaoke building. The ward called her this afternoon. They want the drawings — the layout, the stairs, all of it. She hasn't been asked for those since the fire inspection. And they told her to expect a visit after."

He was not performing grief; he was performing proof. His voice stayed low enough that the room had to lean in, and he kept one hand flat on the counter as if the laminate were the only witness that would not later change its mind. The aunt had kept the building's papers since before the nephew learned to pour beer. The ward had never cared about the stair until somebody made the stair a question. Now the question had a shape: paper requested, visit promised, a file moved from one desk to another. Gao Qiang watched the nephew's audience nod not because they believed him, but because they recognized the shape. On this street, an inspection was never only an inspection. It told you who would be allowed to stand where when the real men arrived.

Gao Qiang carried it up the street under the buzz of the signs, turning it the way his father had taught him — not what happened, but what everyone would do next. The aunt was an old woman, and old women obeyed paper; she would hand the drawings over. A clerk would fetch them. And somewhere behind the clerk, a folder that had been set on a low shelf had been taken down and stamped — because a woman with questions couldn't open a records room, and a stamp could. Somebody up her chain had shelved her once; that was his guess, and shelving was what chains did. Something had knocked the box loose anyway.

He had her filed as patient. She let things go, and she kept them. Now something she kept carried an official mark, and official marks walked into buildings that questions couldn't.

He did not slow his pace in front of the karaoke building.

The interface had nothing to say about any of it. It watched the five of them; the city did not report to it.

Wednesday, quarter past eleven, the apartment smelled of the kettle and yesterday's rice. Kenji sat with his coat over the back of the chair, still in his work shirt, his notebook squared beside his elbow. Kojima was on the floor with a roll of tape, wrapping a knuckle that had no cut in it. Sato stood by the window with the street's glow behind him.

Gao Qiang laid the day flat for Kenji first — the uniforms, the nephew, the drawings — and Kenji listened with his pen uncapped.

"Tomorrow, the envelope goes up at the same minute as last Thursday," Kenji said. "That minute only works if nothing on these blocks is worth a second look. This week the ward is pulling the drawings on the karaoke building. Open the basement now and you put a crowd at that exact address. A noise complaint with a stamp does what her canvass couldn't — it makes the room official. And nothing here can grow past a dead report and a tenth of the margin. You know whose ceiling that is."

"I said I was getting tired of winning," Kojima said, not looking up from the tape. "That was a joke for a man with income." He stretched the tape and tore it with his teeth. "One and a half million doesn't blink. It doesn't take days off. I'm not asking you to light it. I'm telling you what dark costs."

"The crowd won't stand in the street and wait," Sato said from the window. "There's a room north of the river runs Fridays. No lists, no rules. The kind of door where nobody checks teeth." A pause. "And they remember faces. Three winters of winner's cuts — Kojima's face lives in a hundred phones."

"Nobody films the loser," Kojima said.

"They film the hands."

Kojima did not answer. He looked at the wrapped knuckle as if it belonged to a younger, stupider employee. The money was not abstract to him; it had a calendar, a temperature, and a set of faces at a clinic counter that did not take promises. A week of dark was not rest; it was a week the debt grew while his hands sat idle. Gao Qiang watched the cost land and did not interrupt.

Mei's message came while the kettle climbed back to the boil: one word. *Dark.*

Gao Qiang let the positions stand side by side and looked at them without sentiment. To open the stair this week was to take income at the hour it cost the most — with the street below waiting for any motion out of them and the ward above asking for a premise. To leave it shut was to bleed Kojima by the week and lend the crowd to a door that might keep it. Saturday would bring Kenji's pay in and month's end would take the rent out, and the pool would hold its shape through both — but it was not built to carry a debt like Kojima's on top of its ordinary work. What the pool couldn't do, a date could.

"Dark through the weekend," he said. "That stair stays shut while the drawings move. Monday we sit down again. If the paper's gone nowhere by then, we don't relight that stair at all — we find another door, and it isn't on these blocks."

Kojima's jaw worked once. "You're asking me to sit on my hands on a street that watched me use them."

"I'm asking you to be worth more than a story," Gao Qiang said. "Monday has a date on it. That's the difference between it and never."

Kojima looked at the tape for a moment, then put it back in his bag, and for the first time that evening his hands were still. "Monday," he said, the way a man breathes out before he takes a hit.

Sato turned from the window. "There's a print shop two stops north. Empty a year. The door faces an alley, not a street." He left his coat on the hook. "I'll walk it tomorrow, in the light. Alleys read different after sunrise. If the door's still useful, I'll know before Monday."

Kenji closed the notebook, aligning its edge with the edge of the table, and kept his seat. He checked his watch once — tonight a rehearsal for tomorrow's bell. "Boring," he said from the table, "to the second."

Kenji took the notebook to the back room. Kojima folded out the futons in their rows, and Sato took the one by the window, face to the street. The apartment went quiet by degrees.

Gao Qiang opened his own notebook, the cheap one with three headings, and under GUESSED wrote: *the stamp moves in days, not hours.* He didn't write Monday down. Monday he meant to keep in his body, like the sheet.

Tomorrow, one envelope, one bell, one minute. Monday, a door he had never seen. He turned off the light and let the street hum against the glass.
