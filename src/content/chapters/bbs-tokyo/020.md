Saturday, and the rent went out first.

Murakami Kenji counted ninety thousand from the pool at the low table, squared the notes into a band, and handed the packet across without ceremony.

Gao Qiang carried it down four flights. The stairwell held the morning — grill smoke drifting up from the ground floor, laundry steam off the second landing — and at the bottom the building manager stood in his doorframe beside the mail slots, slippers on, a radio murmuring behind him. He counted the ninety thousand where he stood, thumb fast, lips moving. Then he pressed his receipt pad against the wall, wrote through the paper, and held it out. Not a word past the count. Gao Qiang stood through all of it, took the receipt, and climbed back up.

Kenji entered the rent and laid the receipt flat beside the notebook.

Mei came before eleven, ahead of her shift. Her thirteen went down on the table — one ten-thousand note, three one-thousand notes — and Kenji took them and counted them once.

She was at the door with her coat on when she said it. "Tomorrow. The house man doesn't sit in." She went down the stairs for Ramen Ya.

Kenji went alone in daylight and came home with one line.

"Two envelopes. Nakamura's own hands. The wage — and beside it, the month's tenth."

The wage went into the notebook in its turn. "One hundred thirty-six thousand five hundred," Kenji said, and shut the notebook on it. "The pool, as it stands."

The second envelope he set on the low table, unopened, squared to the table's edge. Nobody touched it.

"It never touches the notebook," Gao Qiang said.

Kojima Haruto sat at the table wrapping his hands for tomorrow — cloth, tape worked tight with his teeth — and said nothing about the envelope at all. After dark, Sato Kenjiro put it inside his jacket and went out alone.

---

Saturday, nine at night, and the stair to the zone office was empty.

Sato went up it alone. Endo's runs were done by eight; the landing below the door held nothing but the ghost of old cigarettes. He knocked the way he had knocked the first time, and the door that had opened for Yuzawa opened.

The office was thin. One lamp lit in back, the pot going on its burner, the desk bare but for its book. Hayashi Makoto looked at his face first, the way he had at the first tea, then nodded him in.

Sato put the envelope on the desk and sat.

Hayashi poured two cups and set one at Sato's elbow. Then he opened the envelope where it lay and counted what was inside, the bills flat under his thumb.

"Sixteen thousand five hundred."

He counted it a second time, wrote one line in the book, and closed it. "The month closed clean," he said. "Nobody above has asked anything."

He drank. Sato held his cup and did not drink.

"How is the count treating your old street?"

"Boring."

"Keep it that way."

The pot ticked on its burner. Hayashi set his cup down and gave the term the way he gave schedules — plain, with nothing added anywhere.

"What comes to this desk comes with no ask attached. Nothing that comes here grows past this desk. The day it does, I never knew any of you."

Sato nodded. No question came about who stood behind him.

At the door, Hayashi said the last of it. "Come when something is wrong. Or when nothing is. But come."

Sato set his cup down, full, and went. He walked home the long way, past the shrine.

---

Sunday, and Kojima Haruto said little all day.

He ate at noon. He slept an hour in the afternoon. He rewrapped his hands twice without being asked. Nothing else.

At half past nine, Kojima and Sato went down the stair ahead, together. Gao Qiang gave them their head start, then followed alone — a different way, and into the alley by its other mouth. The print shop's windows were dark. The room above smelled of dust and ink, the presses below silent for the night, one bulb burning over the boards. The bag hung where they had hung it, and the two coffee cans stood on the dead cooler.

At half past ten, the noodle cart rolled out of the alley's mouth, tank rattling over the curb, and the mouth stood empty after it. That was the hour. Sato took the door.

Nobody came before eleven. Then four men, in ones and twos. Each gave a name at the door and Kojima gave it back — the old basement, in the way they took the nod. No bets, no purse, no charge at the door, nothing written anywhere in the room.

Kojima laid the rule down once, flat, before the first pairing. "Nobody bets. Nobody owes. Last exchange at one."

Gao Qiang stood at the wall with a can of coffee going warm in his hand and watched the weather around the bag. Nobody crowded it, nobody talked over the work.

Kojima held the bag for the first man and did not fight. He set it swinging and caught it, palms flat, over and over, and never took a turn himself. Three pairings took the bag and the floor in turn — rounds short, hands light, the boards taking the throws — and nobody left marked. One voice climbed and came back down under Kojima's flat palm on the leather. It did not climb again.

The house took nothing. At one, the room obeyed the hour — coats, nods, gone.

One man turned at the door. "Next Sunday?"

"Ask me on Thursday," Kojima said, and the man laughed once and went.

Gao Qiang, Kojima and Sato walked home carrying nothing. Sato gave his walk on the way, level, in his own order: "Cart rolled at half past ten. Both mouths clean from eleven. Nobody stood around. Nobody came back."

Past two, the lamp was on. Kenji was up — Sunday was his shift at the Goose, home since quarter past eleven — with the notebook closed in front of him.

Gao Qiang gave him the night in one line. "Four through the door. No money moved."

"Nothing to enter," Kenji said, and did not open it.

Sato sat and gave the tea the same way, his own order, flat. "Sixteen five, counted at the desk, twice. One line in a book." A pause. "The month closed clean. Nobody above has asked anything." Another. "The door has a first term. Nothing comes to the desk with an ask attached, and nothing that comes grows past it. If it ever does, he never knew any of us." A last one, quieter. "At the door he said — come when something is wrong. Or when nothing is. But come."

Kojima listened with his wrapped hands open on his knees. Kenji heard it with the notebook still shut. Gao Qiang took the month as it stood: closed clean, the term spoken plainly, and a room over the presses that had held.

The room had held.

Next Sunday, the book opens.
