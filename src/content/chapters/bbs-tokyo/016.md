Chapter 16 — The Answer

Friday, the library kept its own weather. Cold air moved through the stacks in slow tiers, the bound gazettes smelled of warm dust and drying glue, and the loudest thing on the floor was a page turning two desks down. Gao Qiang sat at the carrel he always took, the folder's remains squared in front of him, one hour that belonged to nothing on the blocks.

The trail was more than half burned. Registry pages photocopied years before he ever touched them: THI's consulting-fee structure, two point three billion yen in advisory services paid out across four fiscal years to vendors that had mostly stopped existing. Eleven of fourteen dissolved. Two with representative directors whose registered addresses were mailboxes in Kawasaki. One director dead. The folder read like a fire set by somebody unhurried, and for weeks he had walked its ashes without finding a shape.

Today the shape was small and it had an address.

A quarterly line — advisory fees, six million four hundred thousand yen, the same figure on the same day for nine straight quarters — routed to Hakuun Advisory Y.K., fourth floor of a narrow building in Roppongi, above a bank branch. And on an older page, at the bottom of a dissolution filing, the same building, the same floor, the same room number: the registered office of a company called Tōa Planning, struck off two years before Hakuun ever billed a yen. Two names. One door.

He had walked to the periodicals desk and asked for the box of weeklies from two summers back. The building turned up once in all of it — a photograph of the members' club on its basement floor, three paragraphs about money that moved through the place, and a single line naming an affiliate of the Kuroda group. The story had lived eight days and died without a funeral.

He sat with that for a while, doing nothing with his hands.

A door was not a hand. An address was not a partnership, and a postbox was not a conspiracy — two tenants could share a floor and nothing else. But Zhang Wei's pipeline and the Kuroda group's nightlife had used the same stretch of Roppongi at minimum, and since the night he had started drawing maps at all, his hunt and the street's safety had been two separate sheets. As of a Friday carrel they were one sheet, with a single pin in it.

He fed the copier ten-yen coins, two pages, and the machine gave them back warm. He folded them once and put them in the inside pocket. Three absences sat on his record and the fourth was a door Soo-jin had described without raising her voice; Fridays were the folder, the chair kept its own days, and the arrangement held. The trail was more than half burned and what remained of it would not stretch to waste.

Outside, Friday afternoon had gone to exhaust and grilled eel smoke. Two stops up the line, a report about a game-shop corner sat on a desk in a zone office, and the man behind the desk did not yet know his Saturday question had been answered. Gao Qiang carried the paper home the long way, and the pocket lay flat over his chest like a card he was holding.

---

Saturday came in low and gray, the kind of morning the blocks start late. Sato left before eight, while the shutters were still down and the street hadn't found its coffee. He went alone, in the shirt he wore for the bank runs, and he didn't take the long way to the station.

Gao Qiang sat at the table with tea going cold and made himself useful the only way left to him: he ran the far side of the door. Hayashi had heard by noon yesterday — Endo drove the corner twice, Sato would learn, which is what a zone does when it can't believe its ears. The name would land as one of three things: a favor, a threat, or a currency. Men like Hayashi didn't hear gifts. They heard prices. The only question was who paid this one.

Kojima woke and put the kettle on and said nothing, and the two of them let the clock do the work.

Sato was back before ten with two cans from the vending machine, milk and sugar, and set one down in front of Gao Qiang without a word about it.

"Endo was doing a crossword at the bottom of the stairs," he said, sitting. "Didn't pat me down. That's how I knew I was expected." He opened his can, didn't drink. "Hayashi-san poured the coffee himself. Two cups. We finished the first one not talking about any of it. Then he said, 'You saved me two days. Days are money,' and I gave him the winter."

He said it the way he'd said it Thursday — flat, a fact he was carrying, set down on the table like change.

"Takahashi counting facing the wall. Bags crossing hands once. The Seiko. He listened all the way through and asked one question — why I sat on it all winter. I told him the truth. A collector who tells on a collector doesn't collect long." Sato turned the can a quarter turn. "He said, 'Good. Now you watch, and you say it to me.'"

Gao Qiang kept still. That was the price arriving, and he wanted to hear its exact weight.

"The bounty's dead. The office found the thief, so the street doesn't get paid for the office's work. Takahashi works it off somewhere counts don't reach — he said it like a man naming a bus stop." A breath. "The corner stays Hasegawa's name. The count goes under Endo from Monday. He said a corner is a count, and Hasegawa turned it into a story, and stories are expensive. Then he said the padlock comes off today." The can turned another quarter. "He asked about the tea. It comes at month's end like it's written. He told me never to bring him two things in one visit. Two things is a habit, and habits are what reports are made of."

"And us," Gao Qiang said.

"He knows somebody stands behind me. He didn't ask who. He said to tell them boring suits him fine and asking questions doesn't." Sato looked at the window then, at the corner lights in it. "At the door, he asked if I was eating." He turned the can the rest of the way around and set it down. "The door's still mine. It just doesn't open free anymore."

Gao Qiang ran the table while the can sat sweating between Sato's hands. Okada had paid a hand. Takahashi was paying with the rest of his life, in installments, where nothing counts. Hasegawa had paid the only thing a man on a stool owns — what the corner meant. Hayashi had collected on all of it and spent nothing but coffee. And on their side: one man's boyhood door now carried a tariff, and a boss one layer deep knew there were eyes on his street that weren't his. The name would stay on Hayashi's floor — his line made it business, and business was cleaner. Kuroda's people would get a quiet corner and a tidy report, and nothing in either with a face the five of them owned. The win was that the win had no face.

"We eat," Kojima said, from the stove. Sato almost smiled and drank his coffee, and that was the end of it.

Saturday, Kenji took his wage at the Goose's opening — ninety thousand counted out of the till into a plain envelope, folded twice over, the figure entered in the margin of his pocket book in the same ink as the week before. "Drawer reconciles to the yen," he said, to the register, the way some men say a prayer.

At noon, Mori came back to the game shop carrying the key to the padlock he'd brought in his own pocket Thursday. Gao Qiang watched from across the street, Kenji beside him, up from the Goose with the wage envelope flat in his inside pocket; Kojima had a cigarette going with the floor men at the pachinko hall's side door. Mori took the lock off one-handed and stood in the doorway while Okada ran the shutters up with his left hand, the right splinted flat against his chest. Nobody said anything. The lights came on in a shop where nothing in the window had moved, and the street read it its own way — by two o'clock the version going down the block was that the corner's luck had turned on a Saturday, and that Okada-san had closed three days for repairs no one had seen a trace of.

"Left hand," Kojima said from the side door. "Thirty years sorting with the right." He went back in, and the door closed on it.

Kenji looked once at the open shop and once at his watch. "Monday, the print shop," he said. "My own eyes, then we call the door. The pool carries one week of standing still, and the week is spent."

At three, two ward officials came to the karaoke building with a cardboard tube and a clipboard, and the owner's nephew met them on the pavement with keys on a ring. Gao Qiang took the hour on the wall across the street, a can of vending-machine coffee going cold in his hand. The old man at the basement stair held the whole visit in his hands — he talked for the length of two cigarettes, sketching the stair, the dark, Thursday night on the corner, hands first and words after. The tube stayed leaning by the stairwell the entire time. It went back up the street with the men, and the stair stayed dark behind them.

He came off the corner blocks and ate early at the ramen counter, watching the street through the steam. Mei set down water he hadn't ordered and said only, "Forty minutes," and went back down the counter.

The number ran the way numbers ran on this street. Four hundred had been four thousand by noon; by four, a pachinko runner was telling the taxi rank forty, and that Hasegawa had used a golf club.

---

Saturday night, after ten, Mei came in with the night's leftovers and the building read in full. The ward men had spent their forty minutes in the karaoke building and come out with one question that mattered: who held the basement lease, and had it changed since spring. The old man had answered everything — he was who he was. "And the grandson sat at my counter tonight and ordered nothing," she said, stacking a container on its lid. "He asked what time the koban opens Monday. The weekend he agreed to is up tomorrow night, and he knows it."

She laid a card on the table. Plain, official, one name. Sakamoto.

"She came by at lunch. Didn't ask about hours. Didn't ask who goes down the stair. She asked about paper — leases, management, what changed in the spring." Mei looked at the card without touching it again. "She's stopped looking at doors. She's looking at who owns the dark."

Nobody had seen Takahashi since Friday night. His rooms over the dry cleaner stood dark, and the Seiko had gone wherever wrists go. The street was welcome to its forty-thousand shortage; the number would climb until it found a window, and Monday the window opened at nine.

Gao Qiang closed the week out the way he closed every position — who paid, who learned, what still stood. Okada had his shop back and would count slow for a year. Takahashi was gone where the zone put the men it meant to forget. Hasegawa kept a stool and lost what the stool had meant. Sakamoto had traded a flashlight for a land registry and ended the week richer in questions than anyone on this block could afford. And the five of them stood exactly where they had a week ago — nowhere, in view of no one that mattered, at the cost of a door that no longer opened free.

Then he took the two pages out of his inside pocket and laid them flat under the kitchen light. Hakuun Advisory. Tōa Planning. Same building in Roppongi, same floor, same room, two years apart — and under both of them, faint as a watermark, the name he'd learned to read on these blocks: Kuroda. Zhang Wei's money and Kuroda's nightlife had at minimum shared a door, and that made next Friday's hour the first one with a destination. One look, from the street, in daylight. At a door, not through it.

He folded the pages along their crease and buttoned them back over his chest. Monday had Kenji's eyes on steel in an alley. Month's end had an envelope Sato knew how to carry. Friday had a fourth floor in Roppongi with two names on one door. He put out the light and laid his futon by the window, and the week ended the way he meant to keep ending them — nothing on these blocks pointing at the five of them, and one address, folded small, pointing the other way.
