Chapter 16 — The Answer

Friday kept its appointments in order.

Group 7 met at ten. Park Soo-jin worked down the row with the sheet balanced on her forearm, and Gao Qiang signed the sheet in the second row without slowing his pen. She moved on to Ogawa. Her count held at three. Nothing was said, and nothing needed to be.

The carrel got its hour next — the folder's hour, his own rule, made at this same desk. He kept it the way he kept everything now: exactly, alone, boring to anyone watching.

The THI pages opened to the consulting fees — 2.3 billion yen a year to overseas partners, for consultations no one at THI could describe. He ran the partner names through the registry one at a time. Hakuun Advisory Y.K. held a room in Roppongi. So did Tōa Planning: same building, same floor, same room number. Two companies, one room — a pipe and a door registered over each other. To a policeman the fact meant nothing. To a man reading pipes, it meant the room was where the money arrived and turned into different money — and that the room had an address.

The periodical stacks gave up one paragraph in a dead weekly's bound runs: fringe players around the Kuroda-gumi, and inside it a single line crediting a building-management outfit with Roppongi holdings. He photographed the page flat, no flash. The managing name went into the folder, not onto his lips. Neither did the name at the end of it, which stayed where it had always been — nowhere on paper.

The look planted itself while he squared the pages: daylight, his own eyes, once. Not this week. This week belonged to Monday's review. The folder closed like coursework, and the hour was over.

Friday, quarter past ten at night, Mei came up the stairs with the cold still on her jacket and gave the nephew's report in order, the way she gave everything.

"The visit came. This morning — two men from the ward, with the drawings. They went down and looked at the room." She took the arm of the chair, not the chair. "They left one question on the building. The lease — whose name rents that basement. The family's answer is a management company. Letters to it come back sealed."

A beat.

"Monday, nine o'clock, the koban window. The building's answer gets signed. Signed means it travels."

Kojima turned his cup a quarter and said nothing. Kenji wrote.

"She's done asking people," Gao Qiang said. "People change their stories. Paper keeps one. The room can stay dark — the question that outlives it is who owns it. So it holds. Dark through the weekend. Monday we read what the paper's done, and the venue door moves on Monday's word. Not before."

"The ward's answer," Kenji said. "In writing. Or the shape of its absence."

Mei caught the last train. Saturday's errand belonged to Sato and kept its own hours, and nobody at the table said so aloud.

---

Saturday came up rinsed and bright. Sato Kenjiro walked to the zone office alone, in daylight, the way the assignment read.

The office was thin — one lamp lit in back, the phone unplugged, Hayashi at the desk squaring a week of papers. The tea set sat boxed on the shelf. Neither of them looked at it.

"The count that wouldn't come right," Sato said. "It has a name." He gave it level, no decoration. "Behind the pachinko hall, last winter, the night the hall paid its quarter — Takahashi counted a second stack that never went near the bag. This week he counted the takes wearing a steel Seiko."

Hayashi's pen stopped. He wrote one line and capped it. He took it as what it was — information the street owed the zone — and he didn't ask how Sato had stood behind a pachinko hall on a winter night. He never asked anything that didn't move the work.

"The bounty's dead tonight," Hayashi said. "The zone doesn't bid on its own count." A paper turned, edge to edge. "Takahashi goes somewhere counts don't reach." Another. "Monday, Endo takes the count. The padlock comes off the news agent's. The corner goes back to work."

Sato didn't ask what "somewhere counts don't reach" meant. He had heard words of that shape before, in other offices, about other men. The shape was answer enough.

Hayashi walked him out himself and stopped him at the door with a hand that landed light.

"The door that opened for Yuzawa doesn't open free anymore," he said, gently — the gentleness was what made it expensive. "Names like the one you brought are currency. A door that takes currency has terms. You'll hear them when it matters. Go home in the daylight."

Sato bowed. Outside, the daylight was the same daylight. He walked back the long way, past Okada's chain, and the name he had carried for a winter belonged to a desk now — what it had bought was a Monday he wouldn't stand inside.

---

Saturday night the table was four — Mei home after close, the door's specifics kept from her. Kenji poured tea down the line of cups, and Sato reported standing, spare, in his own order: the bounty dead by nightfall. Takahashi to somewhere counts don't reach. Endo takes the count from Monday. The padlock off Okada's shop.

Then the price, flat, nothing added: the door that opened for Yuzawa doesn't open free anymore. Names are currency. A door that takes currency has terms.

"Okada gets his corner back," Kojima said. "Shame about the hand he'll be sorting with."

Kenji had made the Goose's payday walk on the way home, ordinary as weather; the notebook lay open at the day's line, ninety thousand entered small and square. Under it, Monday held three entries — the window at nine, the ward's answer or its absence, the review that decides whether the venue door moves off-block.

Gao Qiang took the week apart by who held what on Monday morning. Hasegawa kept the sling and lost the count that had justified it, and would only ever read the result. Takahashi was paperwork now. Okada got his corner back with the hand still in a cast. The woman had her window and her question — who owns the dark. The venue kept its dark, the debt kept its weight, and the one door that had ever opened free was free no longer.

Both fronts answered. Nobody had walked free. He had weighed that going in; it sat differently said out loud, with the tea going cold.

The folder stayed on the shelf, the Roppongi page folded inside — an address he had read and never stood in front of. It would keep. Its hour came on Fridays.

"Monday," Kenji said, and closed the notebook.

"Monday," Gao Qiang said.

The week would answer, and he meant to be listening.
