Chapter 10 — The Approach

Saturday arrived without asking permission.

Gao Qiang woke to the particular silence of an apartment full of people trying not to wake each other. Kojima's breathing came from the far corner — deep and even, the sleep of a man whose body was still knitting itself back together. Sato was on his futon near the door, back against the wall, facing the hallway. Same position as yesterday morning. Same instinct.

The kitchen was warm. Kenji sat at the table with his tea, notebook closed beside him. Not reviewing. Not planning. Just sitting.

"Morning," Gao Qiang said.

Kenji looked up. "Morning."

"Nervous?"

"No. I've negotiated with harder people than Nakamura. The hardest negotiation I ever had was with a board director who genuinely believed his own balance sheets were clean. Nakamura knows he's doing something dangerous. That makes him rational."

"You think rational men are easier to deal with."

"Rational men want deals. Irrational men want to prove something." Kenji picked up his cup. "I'll present the evidence, offer the solution, let him see that cooperating is better than the alternative. The hardest part won't be the pitch. It'll be reading his reaction in real time."

Gao Qiang nodded. He trusted Kenji's judgment here. Twenty-five years of reading balance sheets and the people who filed them. If anyone could hold the room with Nakamura, it was the senior accountant who'd spent two weeks learning the man's bar from the inside.

Sato appeared in the doorway. The bruising had shifted — purple fading toward yellow around the edges, the swelling down enough that both eyes were fully open. He'd found one of Gao Qiang's spare shirts, dark blue, too wide across the shoulders, but clean. He'd folded the sleeves twice.

"Morning," Sato said.

"Still sleeping at the door."

"Old habit."

"It'll pass."

Sato didn't argue. He crossed to the kitchen and poured himself water, movements careful, economical. Still learning how much space he was allowed to take up.

The knock came at 8:47.

Mei stepped in without waiting for an invitation, slightly out of breath, a plastic bag in each hand. "Before my shift." She dropped the bags on the table. "Sato — I didn't know your size, so I guessed. If anything doesn't fit, I'll exchange it."

Inside: two t-shirts, a pair of jeans, a light jacket. Not new — but clean, well-maintained, the kind of clothes from a secondhand shop with standards. She'd picked them herself, probably between tables during yesterday's shift.

Sato stared at the pile. He picked up one of the t-shirts, checked the size.

"How much was this?"

"Don't worry about it."

"I can't just—"

"You can," Mei said. "You did. It's done." She was already heading for the door. "I'll see you all tonight. I'll be at the Goose after my shift."

She was gone before Sato could argue. He looked at Gao Qiang.

"She does that," Gao Qiang said. "Don't fight it."

Sato folded the t-shirt carefully and set it beside his futon. Something in his expression had shifted — not gratitude exactly, but something adjacent to it. The look of a person who'd forgotten that people did things without asking for something in return.

Gao Qiang let it sit. No commentary needed.

---

Mei had already left for Ramen Ya after dropping off the clothes — her shift started at ten. Kojima went to the rooftop to do his breathing exercises and didn't come down. Sato stayed inside — his orders were clear: don't exist today. Not on the street, not near the Goose, not anywhere Hasegawa's people might spot him.

Kenji left at 1:45. He put on his good shirt — the one he wore to job interviews, back when job interviews were still a thing he did — and checked his reflection in the bathroom mirror. Adjusted his collar. Straightened his shoulders.

He looked like what he was: a professional accountant walking into a meeting he'd prepared for. Nothing more, nothing less.

"Call me after," Gao Qiang said.

"I'll call you after."

The door closed. Gao Qiang was alone.

He made tea. Sat at the table. Opened the system.

No progress update. No change. The system tracked what it tracked — brothers, loyalty, abilities. It didn't track what was about to happen in a bar on a Saturday afternoon.

Gao Qiang closed the interface and waited.

---

Kenji walked to The Golden Goose at 2:15 PM.

Saturday afternoon in Kabukicho was the neighborhood between masks — the morning workers gone home, the evening crowd not yet arrived. Neon was on but the streets were half-empty, as if the city was catching its breath between performances.

He knew these streets now. Two weeks of the same route, five days a week, had given him a mental map — the alley where the delivery trucks idled, the corner where the tout always stood, the exact distance from the apartment door to the Goose's entrance. Nine minutes if he walked at his normal pace. Today he walked at nine.

The bar was quiet when he pushed through the door. Afternoon light filtered through windows that were never fully clean. Three customers at the counter — regulars, the kind who came for the cheap shochu and stayed because leaving required deciding to go somewhere else. Nakamura was behind the bar, polishing a glass with the focus of a man who'd been doing it for thirty years.

"Murakami." Nakamura didn't look up. "Off today."

"Here for my pay." Kenji took his usual seat at the end of the counter.

Nakamura grunted, set the glass down, and reached beneath the counter. The envelope appeared — standard white, unmarked, the weight of ninety thousand yen in cash. He slid it across the wood.

"Count it if you want."

"No need." Kenji pocketed the envelope. Didn't move.

Nakamura noticed. His eyes — small, dark, set deep beneath a heavy brow — tracked Kenji's stillness. "Something else?"

Kenji placed his hands flat on the counter, palms down. The posture he'd used in a hundred audit rooms. Not aggressive. Patient. *I'm not going anywhere, and you already know why.*

"Two years," Kenji said. "You've been underreporting the weekly gross by approximately five percent. Roughly forty thousand yen per week. Total accumulated over twenty-four months: four point two million."

Nakamura's hands stopped moving. The glass stayed where it was.

"That's a precise number."

"I'm a precise person."

The silence held. Kenji counted three heartbeats, then continued.

"Your method is straightforward — adjust the gross figure before calculating tribute. The records you send upstream are detailed enough to avoid flags but sloppy enough to fail any professional audit. I found the discrepancies in four hours."

Nakamura's face settled into something unreadable. Not anger — Kenji had seen Nakamura angry, the sudden flare that sent bartenders scrambling. This was different. Controlled. Measured. The expression of a man deciding what to do next, not a man who'd been caught off guard.

"I'm not here to expose you," Kenji said. "I'm here with a better arrangement."

"Talk."

"I can clean your books. Not the fake ones — the real ones. Clean enough that no one upstream ever looks twice at this bar. In return, my people manage your accounting going forward. We take a percentage of the operational savings — the waste you're currently losing to inefficiency. You keep your bar. You keep your tribute. You keep everything."

"And if I say no?"

Kenji had prepared four answers for this question. He chose none of them.

"Then nothing changes. You continue skimming, I continue knowing, and eventually the wrong person notices the same discrepancies I did. Not today. Maybe not this year. But the math doesn't favor you forever."

A risk. Gao Qiang had argued for a softer approach — emphasize the benefits, don't threaten. But Kenji had sat across from enough cornered executives to know that a man like Nakamura respected strength more than courtesy. The old man had survived two years of stealing from the yakuza. He hadn't done it by being polite.

Nakamura studied him. The scowl was in place, but Kenji noticed something else — a faint crease at the corner of the man's eyes. Not age. Calculation. Nakamura was working through the angles faster than expected.

"How many people know?"

"Myself and one other."

"Names."

"No."

Nakamura's lip twitched. Not quite a smile. "You're either very brave or very stupid, Murakami."

"Practical. Practical people tend to survive."

Another silence. Longer. Nakamura poured himself shochu — two fingers, no ice. Drank it in one swallow. The glass hit the counter with a crack.

"Come back tomorrow," Nakamura said. "With the details in writing. I want to see exactly what you're proposing."

Kenji stood. "You'll have it by afternoon."

He left The Golden Goose at 3:02 PM. The sun felt warmer than it should have. The negotiation had gone exactly as planned — evidence presented, solution offered, agreement in principle. Clean. Professional. The old man had been rattled but cooperative.

Kenji walked home and did not notice that Nakamura had never once looked surprised.

Mei locked the Ramen Ya back door at 10:03 PM.

Saturday evening was always the longest shift — the post-dinner rush that started at six and didn't thin until after nine. Her feet ached. The collar of her uniform smelled like pork broth no matter how many times she washed it. She changed behind the restaurant, swapping the uniform for jeans and a dark cardigan, pulling her hair back.

The Golden Goose was a twelve-minute walk. Saturday night Kabukicho was the full performance — neon blazing, music bleeding from doorways, clusters of salarymen already unsteady on their feet. Mei moved through it the way she moved through everything: watching without looking, cataloging without stopping.

She'd been inside the Goose twice before, both times during Kenji's shift, both times as Murakami's friend stopping by. Tonight she was nobody. Just a woman at the bar on a Saturday night, ordering a whisky she had no intention of finishing.

The bar was busy. Fourteen customers, two bartenders on shift, Goto at the door. Nakamura behind the counter, working the usual rhythm — pour, serve, collect, pour. Normal Saturday night.

Mei took a seat at the far end of the counter, two stools from the wall. Good sightline to the entrance, partial view of the hallway leading to the back room. She ordered her drink and settled in.

Thirty minutes passed. She nursed the whisky, watched the crowd cycle through, listened to conversations that meant nothing. Two salarymen arguing about baseball. A woman in a red dress waiting for someone who wasn't coming. The bartender making small talk with a regular about fishing.

Then Nakamura's rhythm changed.

It was subtle — a shift in his shoulders, the way his eyes flicked toward the hallway. He said something to one of the bartenders and walked toward the back. The door opened, light spilled out, closed behind him.

Mei's fingers tightened on her glass. She didn't look up. Didn't move. But something at the edge of her awareness sharpened — that low hum she'd learned to trust, the sense that the room had just shifted in a way that mattered.

She counted. Five minutes. Eight. Twelve.

Then she saw it through the window — a black sedan pulling up to the curb.

She knew that car. She'd logged it in her notebook three times: Thursday nights, 1 AM, tribute pickup. The Thursday collector stepped out. Same narrow face, same dark coat. Thursday was collection day. This was Saturday. The collector did not come on Saturdays.

He didn't approach the bar. He walked past the entrance, past Goto at the door — who gave him a small nod, an acknowledgment that told Mei this was not a stranger — and went straight to the hallway.

The door opened. Closed.

Mei sat very still.

She didn't know who the collector was meeting or what they were discussing. Could be a schedule change. An unscheduled payment. A dozen innocent explanations.

None of them fit.

Kenji had been in this bar seven hours ago. He'd laid out the offer. Nakamura had agreed to hear the details tomorrow. And now, on a Saturday night, the tribute collector was in Nakamura's back room.

Mei finished her drink in one swallow. Left a thousand-yen note on the counter. Walked out without looking toward the hallway.

She was two blocks away before she pulled out her phone.

"It's Mei."

"Talk." Gao Qiang's voice was flat.

"Kenji left the Goose around three. The approach went ahead. Nakamura agreed to hear the proposal tomorrow — that's what Kenji reported."

"What else?"

"I went in after my shift. Around ten-fifteen, Nakamura went to the back room. Ten minutes later, the Thursday collector arrived. Black sedan, same face from my surveillance notes. He went into the back room with Nakamura. I left before he came out."

Silence on the line.

"The Thursday collector. On a Saturday."

"Yes."

More silence. Longer.

"Go home, Mei. Don't go back to the Goose tonight."

"Understood."

The line went dead. Mei walked the six blocks to her apartment, locked the door, and sat in the dark for a long time.

---

Gao Qiang set the phone down.

The apartment was quiet. Kojima asleep in the far corner. Sato curled at the door, back to the wall. Kenji was in his space, still awake, reviewing notes from the afternoon — the wording he'd used, the sequence of Nakamura's responses.

Kenji looked good. Relaxed. A man satisfied with a day's work.

He didn't know about the sedan yet.

Gao Qiang opened the system.

*Status:* Active
*Objective:* Gain operational control of The Golden Goose
*Deadline:* Sunday (end of day)

No change. No alert. No threat assessment flagging Nakamura as a variable. The system tracked missions, brothers, loyalty, abilities. It didn't track human deception. It didn't predict that a man who'd survived two years of skimming from the yakuza might have plans of his own.

The system was a tool. Powerful, precise, and limited.

Gao Qiang had relied on it for sixteen days. It had given him information, abilities, a framework for building the brotherhood. But it didn't know Nakamura. Kenji thought the man was a temperamental fool with sloppy books. The system had no opinion at all.

And now the tribute collector was sitting in Nakamura's back room on a Saturday night, seven hours after Kenji walked out thinking he had a deal.

The approach went too smoothly.

Gao Qiang replayed Kenji's report. *He asked how many people know. He asked for names. I said no. I told him one other person and no names. He accepted that.*

Sharp questions. Not the questions of a stupid man. Kenji had described Nakamura as "controlled, measured." At the time, Gao Qiang had read that as a man accepting reality.

Now it read like a man buying time.

He turned to Kenji, who was still awake at the table, going over figures in his ledger.

"What is it?"

"Mei just reported from the Goose. The Thursday collector showed up tonight. Went into the back room with Nakamura. Stayed at least twenty minutes — she left before he came out."

Kenji was quiet. The silence stretched — five seconds, ten, fifteen.

"He asked me how many people knew," Kenji said slowly. "And he accepted my answer too easily."

"Yes."

"He was never going to accept. He was gathering information."

"Probably."

"Then tomorrow—"

"We adjust. We don't walk in blind." Gao Qiang's voice was steady. The framework still held. Information was the weapon. They just didn't have enough of it.

"What do you need?"

"A clearer picture of who Nakamura talks to above the collector. And we need it before tomorrow afternoon." He paused. "Get some rest. We meet in the morning."

Kenji closed his ledger and nodded.

Gao Qiang sat alone in the dark kitchen. The system interface faded from his vision. Sunday was the deadline. Less than a day. They'd planned for Nakamura to be a problem they understood.

They'd been wrong.

He'd been wrong. The system gave him edges — perception, analysis, a map of human potential. But it couldn't read a man who'd spent thirty years teaching people to underestimate him.

First mistake. It wouldn't happen again.

He opened his notebook and began to work.
