Thursday, and the presses were running under the boards.

The room over the print shop held daylight thinly — one gray window, bare boards, the rollers going beneath everything, a steady shudder climbing the beams into the balls of Gao Qiang's feet, the air warm with ink and solvent. Kojima Haruto stood beside the bag and did not touch it. Sato Kenjiro stood at the window, hands in his pockets.

"The man wants his word tonight," Kojima said. "It travels down through the regulars on its own. Before it goes — what the old book took." He gave it flat. "A tenth off every winning bet, off the top. The purse came out of what was left. House ate first, fighters ate last." A pause. "A man who lost his stake got carried to the next Sunday, and the carry had its own rate. I watched men work off one bad night for half a year. I paid that tenth two years myself. I know what it weighs."

Sato spoke once, from the window. "One thing, from my time on that street. The old book kept no paper. One man's head and a roll of thousands in his coat. The night a count came up short, the room took the difference out of whoever stood nearest. Didn't matter who. Nearest."

Gao Qiang turned the position over. A book was what made a room visible: money that changed hands had a mouth, and a mouth got repeated — over a counter, over a bowl, through a nephew to a window. The old room's carry was the loudest thing in it, and loud rooms drew walkers. A rate this small and this clean gave nobody a story worth carrying home.

He gave the ruling at the wall. "The house takes a tenth from the winners only. Nobody owes this house at the end of a night — not one yen, not ever. And nothing is written. The book is Kojima's head. The count gets said out loud once, at the end of the night, and then it's done."

Kojima named his price without being asked. "While I hold it, I don't fight."

Sato gave the door its terms. "Blocks seven to ten, no. Any face from the Goose, no. A man who asks twice after a no stays out."

Gao Qiang took both, and nobody argued. Kojima put his palm flat on the bag and pushed once; the chain creaked over the beam and it swung wide and came back, and he caught it with both hands and held it still.

---

Sunday, and Thursday's word had gone down through the regulars.

Gao Qiang walked his own way in, last, up the outside stair with the iron rail cold through his glove. The presses below were still, the ink smell gone flat overnight, the bulb over the boards the only light. At half past ten the noodle cart rolled out, and the alley's mouth went dark and empty behind it. At eleven Sato took the door.

They came in ones and twos, more than the first card had drawn. Eight men through the door, each name spoken at the sill and given back by Kojima, none of them loud.

Kojima laid the rule once, flat, before the first pairing. "The house takes a tenth from the winners. Nobody owes the house. Last exchange at one." One man turned his banded stake over in his fingers; another pulled his coat off. The room held.

The book lived where the ruling had put it, a roll against Kojima's ribs and the count in his head, and he did not fight. Three pairings took the floor in turn — rounds short, hands light, the throws landing soft on the boards, nobody marked. The money moved small and quiet: a few thousand at a time, bet man to man, the stakes handed to Kojima before each round and paid back after, and coming off the floor each winner peeled the tenth off what he'd won and set it in Kojima's palm.

Gao Qiang stood at the wall and watched the hands. Every note that changed hands was a face that would remember this room, and eight faces were a weight the room would carry every Sunday after. He held the weight where he wanted it: small.

At one the room obeyed the hour — coats, a nod at the sill, gone.

Under the bulb, in the empty room, Kojima said the night out loud once, the way the ruling said to — stakes held, stakes paid, tenth taken — and the three of them walked home through the cold with the roll against Kojima's ribs and the pachinko strip's far chirp thinning to nothing behind them.

Sunday past two, and the lamp was on.

Murakami Kenji was up — his Goose shift, home since quarter past eleven — the notebook shut at his elbow. Kojima set the roll on the low table, squared to the edge, and gave the night in his own order.

"Eight through the door. Names given, names given back. Rule laid once. Three pairings, light. Nobody marked. Stakes held before, paid after." He set the figure down last, flat. "Winning bets came to forty-four thousand across the room. A few thousand a man, three pairings. The tenth is four thousand four hundred." A beat. "Small money."

Sato gave the door in his own order. "Cart rolled at half past ten. Door opened at eleven. Every face that came was a face I knew. Nobody stood around after."

"Thursday we set the rate," Gao Qiang said. "A tenth from the winners only. Nobody owes the house at the end of a night. Nothing written — the book is Kojima's head."

Kenji drew the roll to him and counted it at the table, thumb flat, quick. "Four thousand four hundred." He looked up. "Where does this money stand?"

"It's the room's," Gao Qiang said. "And the room's money is the pool's."

The notebook opened on the room for the first time. "One hundred thirty-six thousand five hundred, plus four thousand four hundred." The pencil ran and came off the page. "One hundred forty thousand nine hundred." He set the pencil down. "It goes in as its own line. The room's."

Kojima named the number he had carried since the old room, once, flat. "Set against the million and a half — nothing yet." The notebook closed on it, and nobody argued the point.

Gao Qiang took the night as it stood: a room that had breathed last Sunday and held a book this one, small, quiet, paid for out of winners' pockets.

"Next Sunday," he said. "Eleven. The door opens, and the book with it."

The room had earned its first money, and it came from winners.
