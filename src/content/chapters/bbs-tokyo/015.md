Chapter 15 — The Count

Thursday, midday, two stops up the line, the city smelled of tonkatsu oil and warm rail steel, and Sato Kenjiro walked like a man with nowhere to be. Gao Qiang hadn't seen that on him in a week. Out from under the blocks, the shoulders came down; nobody on this street had a use for his face.

They had left by the side streets, the long way that skips the corner, and on the walk to the station Gao Qiang had taken the corner's court in without slowing: Hasegawa on his stool with the arm still in its sling, one man at the shutter, and Mori at the curb with his face turned to the foot traffic, talking to nobody. Asking after Sato had been work, and Mori had never loved work. Waiting was cheaper. A man who stops asking has decided to let the answer walk past him.

The print shop sat where the shopping street gives up on itself — steel shutter, a realtor's sheet gone gray behind the glass, a year of sun on the tape. Sato walked the block around it twice, then took him down a concrete arch at the side, into an alley that ran clean through to the next street over. The door was steel, unpainted, facing nothing but back walls and a coin parking lot. A noodle cart's propane tank stood chained at the alley's mouth. One high window above, shuttered like everything else. Sato read the realtor's number off the sheet twice, closed his eyes, and had it.

"Door's good," he said, and put his palm flat on the steel the way he'd touched the apartment door his first night. "Alley runs through — you come out two streets over and nobody's facing you. Landlord's a company, not a man. A company is a phone number, not a favor." He nodded at the arch. "Cart's there after six, every night. Eyes, and no lens on them."

Gao Qiang stood under the arch and ran the street forward, the way he ran everything: who comes, who watches, who leaves first. A crowd that arrives in ones and leaves in twos. Police walk streets; nobody patrols an alley that smells of propane and mop water. Monday, Kenji comes and puts his own eyes on it. Decisions this size get two pairs.

On the southbound platform Sato bought two coffees from the machine, the milk-and-sugar ones, and said nothing about it, and Gao Qiang drank his hot and let him have ten more minutes of being nobody.

Thursday, dusk came down blue between the signs. They had been home an hour when Gao Qiang went out onto the roof to put eyes on the blocks — habit now, the way checking a lock is habit — and Sato followed him up with the day still loose in his shoulders.

The corner by the game shop had changed. The shutters were half down in daylight, wrong for a shop that ran to nine. The court was all there: Hasegawa on his stool, one man at the shutter, Mori at the curb. And the game shop's old man was on his knees on his own pavement with his till drawer turned out beside him, coins and a cup of pens in the spill of a doorway light, counting aloud.

The enhancement caught the corner at the far edge of its reach and pulled the words up the street to him.

"—two thousand four hundred."

"Again."

"I paid Tuesday. Both collections. The full—"

"Tuesday's money reached me Tuesday." Hasegawa's voice stayed conversational, which was the worst thing about it. "What's on the pavement is today's. Count what's in front of you."

The old man counted again. He had sorted game cartridges across that counter for thirty years; small exact things were what his hands knew. Hasegawa watched him the way a customer watches a scale.

"You lost count," he said finally. "Counting is hands. Yours are tired." He nodded once.

Mori came off the curb, took the old man's right hand, laid it flat on the shutter's lower rail, and put his heel through it, once. The sound was small, a dry click, nothing a passing car would catch. The old man made a sound like a question and folded around his hand.

Hasegawa crouched to him, close, and the enhancement thinned but held. "I'm not angry at you, old man. Short is short. Somebody on this street is robbing me." He waited until the eyes came up. "You find me who. Then we talk about your hand, and the shop opens."

Mori ran the shutters to the ground and locked them with a padlock he had brought in his pocket. "Closed," Hasegawa said to the street, not loudly, "till the count comes right." Then the court picked itself up and moved off toward Ichibangai, unhurried, moving like weather.

The street unclenched by degrees. Two men from the pachinko hall helped the old man up and walked him toward the clinic on the north corner, and nobody looked at anybody. Forty meters up the block, the basement stair under the karaoke building sat dark with a ward visit promised on top of it, and the old man who kept that doorstep had watched every second of the count. He was the kind of man who answers questions when the men holding them carry clipboards.

"Okada-san," Sato said. Not loud. His knuckles had gone the color of the rail. "He poured me barley tea all through summer. Like I was a customer."

There was nothing to say back that Sato didn't know, so Gao Qiang said nothing, and Sato let go of the rail one finger at a time and stood there and took it.

Gao Qiang ran the corner the way he had to. A boss who can't say *short* up the chain holds a theater instead: the street lies, the count is wrong, the fist is right. But sealing shutters kills the fee — he isn't collecting, he's performing, and the run is booked past the point the street can pay for seats. And tonight the shortage stopped being private. Hasegawa had put a bounty on his own thief in front of forty witnesses. Every shutter on these blocks would go hunting the same name tonight, and most of them would hunt it loud. By Monday, a stamped file and a street full of witnesses were going to find each other. Hasegawa was feeding a front he couldn't see.

Gao Qiang had owned the name since last Friday, held it out of the air and off the page. Tonight it had a clock on it.

Thursday, quarter past eleven, the apartment smelled of the kettle and pork broth gone thick at the edges of Mei's containers. She had come in at half past ten with the night's leftovers and stayed, which she did now when the day was bad. The blocks outside had gone polite, the way a room goes polite when somebody has been hit in it, and the politeness was still on the street.

Kojima sat against the wall with his hands open on his knees, which was worse than a fist. "They did his hand," he said. "Thirty years of sorting little plastic cartridges, and they broke the sorting hand. Over a fee that's beer money."

Gao Qiang waited until the breathing in the room settled, then spent the quiet. "The count on that corner has been running wrong longer than the cast has been on, and it is never coming right. It isn't the street."

"Say the rest," Kojima said.

"Takahashi."

"I knew before I left." Sato said it from the window, flat, no excuse in it. "He counts facing the wall. Bags cross hands once — his. I watched him all winter. A collector who tells on a collector doesn't collect long, so I kept it. It was worth nothing then. Just a fact I was carrying."

Kenji had his pen out and didn't use it. "Thirty thousand a week is a hundred and twenty a month. That's a man's wage. Wages get spent, and spending shows." He looked across at Kojima.

"He's wearing a Seiko he didn't own in the spring," Kojima said.

"So the street finds him inside a week — Kojima found him from across a street. Whoever brings Hasegawa the name first owns the favor, and we are not standing in that line." Gao Qiang set his hands flat on the table. "It goes up. Not down."

"Down's faster," Kojima said. "Put it in Hasegawa's ear and watch him eat his own man by Friday."

"And when he's finished, he asks the only question he cares about: who knew the inside of his count. Everything we hold sits on these blocks — a bar that has to stay boring, a street that has to stay under notice. A favor from Hasegawa comes with a hook in it. And a street where men get knelt on sidewalks is a thing that grows. Growing is the one crime the zone named. The office hears about tonight by Friday. Saturday morning it opens onto a question — and the answer walks in wearing the one face the knowledge fits. Somebody who collected next to Takahashi all winter."

"It's my door," Sato said, before the weighing had quite finished. "I'm not settling anything. Takahashi fed me when the crew ate and stole with the same hands. I'm spending what I know before the street spends it worse." He looked at the window and the corner lights in it. "Okada-san poured tea for the collector nobody poured tea for. Let him keep his shop."

"And the clinic bill?" Kojima said.

"Not from us. Money from nowhere is a signature."

"The number on the street tonight is four hundred short," Mei said. "By tomorrow it'll be four thousand. Numbers grow in the dark." She stacked a container on its lid. "Okada's grandson came in at closing. He wanted the koban's number. My boss poured him two beers and talked him into the weekend. The weekend is what he agreed to. Swollen hands don't keep schedules."

"If the dark stair runs past Monday," Kenji said, pen still capped, "the pool carries one week of Kojima's standing still. It carries one. It doesn't carry two."

"Saturday morning, then. Before the street finishes its coffee. The noise takes a day to climb, and the name has to arrive as an answer, not a confession." Gao Qiang looked at Sato. "You go alone. You know the door."

"I know the door."

Tomorrow held its own hour — an old folder, a library desk, a trail cooling at its own patient pace. It was older than all of this and didn't care about the corner, and nobody here could carry that hour for him, the same way nobody could sit his seminar chair. He let it stand where it stood.

Kenji left his pen capped. At half past midnight he buttoned his collar back up and went down to the street — the first bell under the arrangement was not a minute he trusted to anyone else's hands.

Kojima folded out the futons in their rows without being asked. There was a clinic receipt folded small in his jacket pocket, showing at the edge; Gao Qiang saw it and said nothing. Everyone's week had a bill in it. Sato took the futon by the window, face to the street, boots off, day clothes on.

Thursday, one o'clock. The Goose's windows had gone dark at midnight, and the block kept only its timers and the taxi rank at the crossing. Gao Qiang stood on the roof with his hands in his pockets and made himself watch the way he would watch weather: nothing to do, everything to see.

The sedan turned in at the minute it had kept for years and stopped where it always stopped. Kenji came out the side door in his shirtsleeves with the envelope from the drawer beneath the till tray — the same fold, the same thickness as last Thursday's. The window came down two fingers. The handoff ran its forty seconds, no word either way the street could hear. The window went up; the sedan pulled out; the block stayed empty in both directions.

The minute had held on a day that had broken a man's hand on its own pavement. Boring wasn't a wall. It was a position, and tonight the ground under it had not shifted.

He stayed until the sedan's lights went off the block, and then he went down to bed. Two days out, a door only Sato knew, and a knock Sato had been carrying since he was a boy. Monday had its date; Saturday had its hour now. Below, the game shop's shutter held its new lock under the street's small ordinary noise, and the street went on as if nothing down there was counting.
