Chapter 15 — The Count

Thursday came up bright and cold over Ichibangai, the shutters half-raised, the pachinko halls quiet. Sato Kenjiro walked the blocks the way he always walked them in daylight — unhurried, forgettable, a body with somewhere ordinary to be.

He reached the head of the street a little before eleven and saw the truck first, parked crooked at the corner by Okada's news stand. Mori at the shutters. Takahashi at the truck bed. Hasegawa leaning on the fender in his sling, the cast chalk-white in the sun.

Sato stopped at the back of the small crowd. He knew the choreography from the inside; he had never watched it from out here.

Okada counted his tray twice and came up short of the figure Takahashi gave him. Then he made his mistake — he asked what the second collection was for.

Mori put him down on his own pavement, and the heel came down once on the hand he sorted papers with. The sound carried. Forty-odd people stood very still and looked at nothing.

The chain ran through the shutters. The padlock's click dropped into the quiet like a coin.

"Till the count comes right," Mori said.

Hasegawa straightened off the fender and made his voice carry the way his cast couldn't. "My take runs short because somebody in my count is feeding off it. Half a million for the name. Cash. No questions where it came from." He said it to the whole street and watched nobody look at anybody.

At the truck, Takahashi counted the takes with his sleeve turned back, bills flicking under his thumb. Steel took the light at his wrist — a Seiko. Two years of collections, twice a week, and Sato had never seen that watch.

He kept his pace out of the crowd. No face turned after him. Three blocks on, he let the watch come back, turned it over once in his head, and put it away.

---

Thursday night, a quarter past ten, and the apartment smelled of staff ramen nobody had touched. Mei had come up after closing with the container, coat still on. Kojima sat against the wall. Sato laid the morning flat for her: the tray counted twice, the question Okada asked, Mori's heel, the chain, the half million said out loud to forty people. The watch.

The print-shop walk had been Gao Qiang's own correction. At noon he had taken back his ruling from the night before: Monday's review was about the ward's paper, and the street had filed its own, so he sent Sato out the same day.

Mei gave the counter's version. It had run through Ramen Ya all afternoon, forty tellings with the same bones. Okada wrapped the hand in a towel and walked himself to the clinic; the corner sat dark behind its chain.

"The clinic will want paying," she said. "Someone should stand that bill."

"No." Gao Qiang kept his thumb flat on the table until the feeling underneath it passed. "It's not ours. The hand that pays that bill signs something on that street, and the only thing we've ever owned there is no name at all."

Murakami Kenji came in at quarter past eleven with the Goose on his coat. "Bell's at one," he said, hanging it. "Start me at the beginning."

Sato laid the morning out a second time, thirty seconds' worth. Then the walk: the alley door runs through to the service street, clean. The letting sheet in the shop window had gone gray, and the name on it was a management company — a Tokyo office, a logo, nobody from the blocks. After six, a noodle cart set up at the mouth of the alley and faced the door.

"Priced," Kojima said. "Not moved."

"Monday's word still moves it," Gao Qiang said. "Nothing tonight changes that." Then he spent what had stayed off paper since Sato's first night at this table. "The shortage has a name. It's had one since then. Takahashi."

Kenji went very still, the way he did when a thing needed writing down later.

Sato didn't answer at once. His eyes settled on the middle of the table. "The watch," he said, and stopped, and started again. "Last winter. The pachinko hall paid its quarter. I forgot the receipt book, came back for it after. Takahashi was behind the hall with a second stack. It never went near Mori's bag."

"A long time to carry it," Kenji said.

"You said things on that street, you went somewhere."

Kojima turned a palm up. "Half a million says the street wants that name. We could sell it down and take the money."

"Then we exist to Hasegawa. He pays once, then wonders who else watches. And the thief becomes a private debt between us and a man who breaks hands on pavement."

Kojima let the half million go with a shrug. "Worth the asking."

"The name goes up," Gao Qiang said. "Not down. A collector skimming the count is stealing from the zone — that's desk business, not street business. We're not asking anyone for anything. This morning the street asked a question out loud, forty deep. Somebody answers it."

Mei took her cup to the sink, ran water over it, gathered her coat. At the door she looked back once — not at anyone, at the room — and went down the stairs.

"Saturday morning," Gao Qiang said to Sato when her steps had gone. "Daylight. Alone, to the door that opened for the tea. You stood in a crowd this morning like forty others, holding the answer the whole street heard. No ask attached. Nothing of ours in the frame."

"Alone," Sato said. "I know the door."

"One name, no return address," Kenji said. "The zone is owed the information. Delivery doesn't have to say where from."

The week set itself out: the bell tonight, the folder's hour Friday, Saturday's door, Monday's review.

Kenji buttoned his coat and went back down to stand at the minute.

---

Thursday, eleven exactly, and Murakami Kenji counted the till to the yen. The last two customers took their change and their coats. Nakamura Tetsuo squared the week's envelope at the back of the counter, sealed it, and set it under the counter's edge.

Kenji went home for the table and came back after it; the bar sat empty and brown. Nakamura wiped the counter a fourth time, though it didn't need it.

At one by his watch, headlights swung across the window and the black sedan drew to the curb, patient as paper. The narrow-faced collector came to the door. Nakamura lifted the envelope from under the counter's edge and carried it over. The collector took it, counted nothing, gave the small nod he gave, and was in the car. The sedan pulled away fifty-one seconds after it had stopped; Kenji kept the second hand on it the whole way.

Boring, to the second. The arrangement's first bell, rung exact.

Nakamura turned the back-room key once, and that was the night's whole ceremony.

Kenji walked home through the cold with the notebook in his coat. The bell had its line. Under it, one word: Saturday.
