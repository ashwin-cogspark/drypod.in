Chapter 18 — The Deposit

Tuesday opened at nine with the phone face-up on the table, speaker on, so the whole room could hear the man from the management office across the river say the terms in his own voice.

His voice came steady. Sixty-five thousand a month. One month held as deposit, returned when the keys came back. First month due at signing, which put one hundred thirty thousand across the table on the day. No renewal fee. No agent's fee. He could meet at the room at two.

Murakami Kenji read the terms back in his old conference-room voice — sixty-five monthly, one month held against key return, first month at signing, no fees beyond — and the man agreed.

Kojima Haruto asked whether the deposit came back if they walked clean, and got his yes. Sato Kenjiro asked about the alley door's hours and got those. Gao Qiang sat with his forearms on the table, eyes on the phone, and settled inside the minute.

"It fits. We take it at the terms as spoken." A beat. "Two o'clock."

Mei arrived at ten with her day off in shopping bags and cooked the midday meal — simmered pork, rice, miso thick enough to stand a spoon in — the standing promise kept. The terms went around the table again while she ate. She set her chopsticks down.

"A room that cheap on that street is cheap for a reason," she said. "Find the reason before you love the room."

She washed her hands, took her coat, and left for her own apartment ahead of the signing.

At two, a man waited at the alley stairs with a folder of forms. The name Murakami went on the page. Kenji took the seal from his pocket — carried since his first week at Tanaka Heavy Industries, twenty-five years, the carved face gone shallow at its edges — pressed it into the red pad, and set it down on a letting form the way it had once gone down on procurement schedules and year-end approvals. It cost something at the wrist. He let it cost only that.

He counted one hundred thirty thousand onto the table, took the receipt, and received two keys strung on a bare loop of wire. No tag. No name.

The room was bare boards and one window over the service street. Pallets of diet-tea cartons stood along a wall, the last tenant's stock; the cooler in the corner clicked when tried and gave nothing back. He carried the pallets down through the rumble of the presses and traded them across the print shop counter — the two of them standing cartons five a side to count — for two cans of coffee. First words with the downstairs neighbor, fair both ways.

He set the cans on the dead cooler, asked nothing else, and walked home ordinary through smoke and pachinko chirp, sweat cooling under his collar.

---

Wednesday's sheet at Keio took Gao Qiang's signature clean, and Park Soo-jin's pen ran down the column past him without pause. The count held at three. Neither of them said it.

She caught him at the corridor door and spoke at her logistics volume, flat as a timetable.

"The scholarship doesn't pay for itself. I do my uncle's books Sundays — furniture shop, behind the East Gate. Since my first year." No pause. "If you ever need furniture, the student price is real. While the sheet holds."

Then she was gone down the corridor, gone before the offer could settle into threat or favor. He read her the way she had just read him: once, for exits. What she had set down could be a hand or a leash depending on who moved first, and she had left before he could move. He pocketed it unopened.

Late afternoon, daylight still on the alley, Kojima and Sato came up the outside stairs with a secondhand bag between them, chain coiled on top. Thirteen thousand, chain included — the recycle shop two stations down.

"Cheaper than it should be," Kojima said. "Somebody's sweat is in the lining. Good. It's honest that way."

They hung it from the beam. "Beam's good," Sato said. Kojima put his knuckles to the leather once and watched it swing and settle. "Cooler's dead," he said. "This one hits back."

Sato walked the cooler under the window and stood the two coffee cans on it. A shelf. The room smelled of dust and printing ink come up through the floorboards, presses turning below. Gao Qiang tried the lock from inside once — one turn, the tongue solid.

Nothing in the room said what the room was for. A bag, a cooler with cans on it. He let it stay that plain.

---

Wednesday night, quarter past ten, Mei's drop came in order, the way she gave everything. Kojima and Sato were at the table, arms tired from the stairs.

The nephew first: the signed answer had left the koban's box for the ward office's tray the same morning it was signed — the window officer had said as much. Then today's item: the clipboard came back around Ichibangai a second time, shopkeepers initialing again, the family's names among them.

Her read, one line, flat: "Everything comes back as clipboards."

Gao Qiang took it without reaching. The paper hadn't died at the signing. Signed paper travels, and a clipboard round was how a file stayed warm. Nothing to answer tonight. He set it aside the way he set aside weather.

"Last train," Mei said. He walked her to the station stairs. "Eat on your day off," he said, and she snorted and went down into the light.

Kenji came home at quarter past eleven smelling of the Goose — smoke, beer taps, other men's cologne — and the notebook came down onto the table. The line went in small and square: two hundred sixty-six thousand five hundred, less one hundred thirty thousand at the signing — sixty-five first month, sixty-five against key return — less thirteen thousand for the bag. One hundred twenty-three thousand five hundred. He ruled the line closed. Not celebrated. Entered.

The week, laid out once, short. Thursday the sedan, boring to the second. Saturday, wages and the ninety thousand for rent, and the envelope traveling alone — no path named, none needed at this table. Sunday, the card, after the cart goes home.

"Friday," Kenji said. Not quite a question.

"Mine," Gao Qiang said, and Kenji closed the notebook and asked nothing further.

On the shelf the Roppongi page sat folded, two days from its hour. The room over the presses was real, and every hour of the week was spoken for but one. That one was his. He put out the lamp.
