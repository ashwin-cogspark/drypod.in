Chapter 18 — The Deposit

Tuesday morning the letting sheet lay flat on the kitchen table, the phone number circled once in pencil. Murakami Kenji read it through as if the digits might have moved overnight, then dialed at nine exactly and set the phone on speaker, propped on a folded newspaper so the table could hear.

A man answered on the second ring with an office in his voice.

"The unit behind the print shop," Kenji said, and the bank suit came into his voice without the suit. "You have it listed."

"Sixty-five a month." No greeting, no weather. "One month down as deposit — it comes back when the key does. First month at signing, cash at the handover, the receipt book stamps it. Handovers are Wednesdays and Fridays, two o'clock. The unit lets to businesses, so the sheet wants a name and a seal." A pause with paper shuffling in it. "Storage or trade?"

"Trade."

"Waste removal is the tenant's. Wednesday, two o'clock. The name?"

"Murakami."

The line clicked off the way machines do, finished with them.

Sato Kenjiro ate his rice standing at the counter. Kojima Haruto held his tea with both hands and looked at the middle of the table. The name sat in the room with them, and Gao Qiang let it sit. Paper was where the state read a street now — Monday had made that plain — and their own paper was going to carry one of their names, in ink, two stops north of every block that knew their faces.

"It's been on worse," Kenji said, folding the sheet along its old crease. "This one only says tenant."

Mei came in before her shift with her jacket over her arm, heard the last of it from the doorway, and gave them the street instead of congratulations. "The paper went in a box at the koban," she said. "That much everyone agrees on. After the box, the rank says it sits in a ward tray until it turns into a man with a clipboard, and then it sits some more. Nobody knows which tray." She took water from the tap. "The old man at the stairs thinks Takeda's desk. Takeda's desk thinks the moon, probably."

"Either way it comes back as clipboards," Gao Qiang said.

"Everything comes back as clipboards." She shrugged the jacket on. "Not our blocks' problem for a month. Somebody else's street should get a turn."

After she'd gone, Kenji opened the pocket book and squared it on the table, and read the week off it the way he reconciled a drawer.

"Two hundred fifty-seven thousand this morning. Tomorrow, one hundred thirty thousand out at signing. Saturday, ninety out for the rent here, ninety in from my wage, thirteen in from Mei. The envelope takes sixteen and a half out after, on its own. Sunday morning the pool stands at one hundred twenty-three thousand five hundred." He closed the book. "Thin. Positive. It's a number we can meet."

"Then we meet it," Gao Qiang said.

The week would land with almost nothing under it — a position with no depth, where one surprise fell all the way through. He weighed that and set it beside the other weight, Kojima's receipt sitting in its jacket pocket, weighing the same every day it went unanswered. Standing still had weight. They could carry thin for one week. Not two.

At noon he took the roof with Sato out of habit, and the habit had become the shape of the thing: two sets of eyes on one street, one of them free. By ten to twelve the envelopes were out, held in hands and pinned under arms along the alley wall behind Menya Kuro — the street had learned the hour in a single day. The sedan came at twelve exactly. Endo counted in ink, in order. Mori held the bag. Okada paid left-handed, on his own feet, and went back to his sorting.

The stool under Hasegawa's awning stood empty through all of it.

"He's not watching the count," Sato said. "The count doesn't need him. That's the whole change." He watched the trunk lid close. "Nothing to carry this week. Boring."

"Boring's the job," Gao Qiang said.

"Boring's the job," Sato agreed, and his shoulders came down the half inch they'd learned.

---

Wednesday morning the Toyoko line carried Gao Qiang out of the blocks and delivered him to Hiyoshi with the students, and for ninety minutes he was the thing his visa said he was. Group 7's room had morning light and a radiator that ticked. The sign-in sheet came down the row, and he wrote his name in the second column and dated it, the characters even.

Park Soo-jin counted heads with a pencil, not a finger. When she reached his row the pencil made its small mark and moved on without pausing, which was the whole transaction as far as the sheet was concerned.

Ogawa signed the sheet with a flourish that belonged on a menu and told her the class was lucky to have her. She looked at him until he sat down.

Afterward she caught Gao Qiang at the vending machines, stood while the cans dropped, and spoke to the machine rather than to him.

"You signed. Three misses this term, and the floor is eighty. You clear it as long as the chair keeps holding you. That's the number. You only have to not touch it."

"I'm not touching it."

"You've missed three times already." She let that go past and kept to her line. "I keep the room log. Fridays I'm the last one out; I lock up. What you do in those hours, I haven't seen. That's a fact, not a favor." The can cracked open. "Your folder got heavy again last week. I carried it to the shelf and I didn't hold it. That's as far as I go."

"Soo-jin."

"I'm talking." She turned around now. "You want to know where I stand, so I'll say it the once. While the sheet holds you, I'm furniture. I count, I lock up, I forget. The day it breaks, I'm the person they ask first — why I logged the hours and never wondered. I don't stay ignorant for people who aren't in the room." She drank. "Miss again and the letters write themselves, and one of them has my name on it. So be in the chair, and keep letting me not know."

"For what it's worth," Gao Qiang said, "you're the most honest person in my week."

"I know." She was already walking. "That's the problem with furniture. It hears everything and can't repeat any of it."

He watched her go and weighed her the way he weighed positions now: an ally who described herself as furniture, holding a door she'd built herself out of not-knowing. The door would hold as long as he held the chair. Both facts were now the same fact.

---

Wednesday, two o'clock, the shutter street ran its daylight shift — the print shop's roller up, solvent and warm paper breathing into the alley, the alley mouth bare where the cart would sit at six, two oil stains and a boot scrape marking the spot like an anchor's. The company's man was young, with a clipboard, a stamp case, and no intention of crossing any thresholds. The office, he said, didn't enter the units. He read the meter, tried the steel door's locks once each, and stood in the doorway for the money.

Kenji counted it out in ten- and five-thousand-yen notes. "Deposit and the month. Sixty-five and sixty-five." The man wrote the number twice, stamped both lines, tore the carbon, and held out the tray for the seal. Kenji pressed it — he'd carried the seal out of the wreck of a twenty-five-year career the way other men carry a watch — and the room had a tenant, and the tenant had two keys on a ring of bare wire.

Then they opened the door properly, and yesterday's voice from the phone was standing in the room wearing its surprise: pallets of flyers for a diet tea nobody had drunk, three seasons out of date, stacked along one wall, and a drinks cooler humming nothing in the corner.

"Waste removal is the tenant's," Sato read off the wall of it, flat.

The older of the two machine men from the print shop came to his roller, looked at the pallets, and held up two fingers. Paper was paper. Gao Qiang got two cans from the vending machine at the alley mouth and handed them over, and weighed the purchase while he did it: the shop would know their faces, and know them as the ones who'd brought coffee, which was a better thing to be two stops north than a pile of aging paper in a room that needed to stay quiet. The cooler stayed. It was theirs now too.

Sato walked the alley mouth to mouth once and came back with the number. "Minute and a half, at a walk. Less if it matters."

Kojima carried the last pallet one-armed, set it inside the shop's roller, and rolled the shoulder after it, testing the joint the way he tested everything.

"You wanted a body to practice on," Gao Qiang said.

"A cooler holds still." Kojima rubbed the shoulder once and let it go. "Four weeks and change. I need one that hits back."

While Kenji squared the contract into the folder, Gao Qiang stood in the middle of the bare room — one steel door, two ways out through the alley mouths, one wall warm from the machines — and put his hand flat against the inside pocket, where two folded pages had waited out another day. Paper of their own, going down in daylight. He took nothing out. Friday had its own hour, and the room had this one.

---

Wednesday night the kitchen held the four of them and the two keys on bare wire under the stove light, turning a little when anyone bumped the table, catching the light like the only new thing in the apartment.

"When does it open?" Kojima asked.

"Sunday night." Gao Qiang let the week run out in front of him the way it would actually fall. "Tomorrow the sedan, the bell at the Goose. Friday, daylight — a street, a building, one look, and home. Saturday the rent, the wage, and after that the envelope, on its own trip. Sunday carries nothing, so Sunday carries the card — after the cart's gone home." He nodded at the keys. "One card. Small — faces we know, no book the first night. If the room holds quiet till morning, the second one grows."

"It'll hold," Kojima said.

"It'll hold," Sato said, in the voice he used for facts he'd checked.

Kenji copied the contract's number into the pocket book, dated it, and closed the book on a week that would land thin and standing. Outside, the blocks ran their evening — the pachinko churn, the yakitori smoke standing in its own light — and none of it had anything to do with a shutter street two stops north, which was exactly the point of the shutter street.

"Four weeks and change," Kojima said anyway, from the doorway, on his way to the futon like a man keeping an appointment.

Gao Qiang put out the light. Friday first: one door, in daylight, from the street. Then the room could start being theirs.
