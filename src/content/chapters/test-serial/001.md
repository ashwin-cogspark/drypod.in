Kenji Mori had spent eleven years counting things for Marukoshi Trading. Receivables, headcount, minutes until the last train. The world, his mentor used to say, is a spreadsheet that hasn't been formatted yet, and the man had retired at fifty-one with a detached house in Kamakura and a heart stent, which Kenji had come to regard as the two available endings of the profession.

What he had never counted was his own breathing.

He was counting it now because there was nothing else left on the ledger. It was 11:52 on a Tuesday, platform three, Kichijoji station, and the last Chuo rapid was two minutes away according to the board and four minutes away according to the sag in the crowd's shoulders. Kenji knew that sag. He had caused it often enough, standing at the head of a conference room, announcing revised quarterly targets with the gravity of a man reading weather news.

In: four counts. Out: six. The physiotherapist had prescribed the ratio for his blood pressure, along with a lecture about boundaries that Kenji had billed to the company insurance and internalized exactly none of.

The train arrived the way creditors arrive: all at once, and taking more than it gave. Kenji boarded, secured a strap, and closed his eyes to begin the standing nap that was the only meditation he had practiced consistently since 2015.

【LEDGER OF BREATH — ACCOUNT OPENED】

He opened his eyes.

The words hung in the air between his face and the back of a stranger's gray suit, rendered in a thin, precise font he recognized from the company's invoicing software. Gold trim. A small box in the corner read BALANCE: 0 QI.

"No," Kenji said, in the tone he reserved for interns.

The salaryman crammed against his left shoulder glanced over. Kenji bowed an apology of approximately seven degrees and returned to the window, which was still there, and which refreshed, politely, when he blinked.

He understood immediately what was happening. He was having the event. The one human resources had warned about in the mandatory wellness seminar, right after the slide about screen time and before the slide about the employee assistance hotline, which was open weekdays from ten to four, presumably on the theory that collapse keeps business hours. Eleven years of overtime, konbini dinners, and the Chuo line's idea of personal space had finally billed him back.

The rational move was to go directly to a hospital. Kenji examined this move from all sides, the way he examined any contract, and found the termination clauses unacceptable. A psychiatric note in his file would cost him the Fujimoto account, and the Fujimoto account was the last thing in his life with a growth curve.

So he did what he always did with an unexplained line item. He audited it.

Rules of the audit, established over the remaining seventeen minutes of the ride, observed with the detachment of a man narrating his own decline:

The window did not follow his eyes. It stayed where it appeared, relative to him, like a lanyard badge. It did not reflect in the train window, which ruled out, in Kenji's non-medical opinion, most of the retina. Nobody else on the platform had looked at the air where it hung, and Japanese commuters were many things, but unobservant about floating text near their faces was not one of them.

When he held his breath, a small counter in the corner of the window incremented: CYCLES: 0. When he resumed the physiotherapist's ratio — four in, six out — and completed one full cycle, the counter ticked to 1, and a line of gold text slid across the air like a receipt being printed.

【DEPOSIT RECORDED: 1 QI. BALANCE: 1 QI.】

His stop arrived. He missed it on purpose, stayed on the train an extra two stations, and did the arithmetic. The round trip to Nakano and back cost him twenty-six minutes and gained him, if he kept the ratio the entire way, roughly forty cycles. Forty qi. The window helpfully confirmed: BALANCE: 41 QI after the return leg, because apparently it counted the ride out too.

Forty-one qi. The balance bar underneath suggested a capacity of one hundred, and beneath the bar, in smaller text, a line that Kenji read four times before the train reached Kichijoji again.

FOUNDATION ESTABLISHMENT: 0%.

* * *

His apartment was a one-bedroom in Mitaka that he had furnished in the style of a man who planned to be at the office during all hours the furniture might be used. He drank a glass of water, took his blood pressure — 142 over 90, improved, which was infuriating, since it meant the hallucination was good for something — and lay down on a bed he had last made in March.

The ceiling had a water stain shaped like Kyushu. He stared at it and ran the numbers.

If the window was a hallucination, it was the first hallucination in medical history with a consistent double-entry system. Balances carried over correctly. Deposits required completed cycles, not attempted ones; he'd tested that by hyperventilating halfway through a breath near Mitaka and receiving nothing but dizziness. There was even, when he pressed his tongue to the roof of his mouth and exhaled through it slowly — a trick from a YouTube video he had watched at 2 a.m. in a moment he would deny in court — a modifier.

【RATIO BONUS: +10% DEPOSIT. TECHNIQUE REGISTERED: NAMELESS BREATHING (IMPERFECT).】

Imperfect. The word bothered him more than the floating text did. It bothered him the way a misaligned column bothers a man who has spent a decade aligning columns.

He turned off the light. In the dark of his bedroom, the window glowed faintly gold, patient as an unpaid invoice.

Kenji Mori, thirty-four years old, senior account manager, divorced, blood pressure 142 over 90, had one talent, and it was not qi. It was the willingness to sit with a spreadsheet until it confessed.

"Fine," he told the ceiling, and began, deliberately, four counts in, six counts out, to breathe.

【ACCOUNT ACTIVE. BEGIN DEPOSITS.】

By 1:00 a.m., his balance was 217 qi, and somewhere below his sternum, where he had never bothered to feel anything except heartburn, something warm had started, very faintly, to turn.
